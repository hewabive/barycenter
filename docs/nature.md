# Nature

The model of nature behind the generator of star systems: what it makes, by which rules and numbers, and what it leaves out. Each number says where it comes from: a published work, cited by authors and year and listed in `sources.md`, a check against known bodies, "chosen for play", or "unchecked" where its origin is not yet traced. A mark at the end of a sentence covers the numbers of that sentence that have no mark of their own. Constants of nature and what follows exactly from a law need no mark.

- A system is made by the core from the seed of the world, in a stream of random numbers of its own, so a change of the generator leaves the cells and regions of a seed where they were. The seed of the home body is drawn first. Names, the seeds of the other bodies and their places on their orbits come from a second stream, so a change of the physics leaves the names of a system alone as long as it has the same bodies.
- The core also makes a system with no home, for the generator page and later for other stars. Its star is drawn between 0.08 and 2.5 solar masses unless one is given, and seven times in a hundred its centre is a brown dwarf of 0.013 to 0.074 instead (chosen for play), the least masses that burn deuterium and hydrogen being 0.013 and 0.07 to 0.075 (Chabrier and Baraffe 2000). Its orbits number up to twelve and nothing on it is alive (chosen for play).
- A system has an age: 2 to 8 billion years with a home and 0.1 to 10 without, and for a star in its prime never more than four fifths of the time it spends on the main sequence (chosen for play), which is 10 billion years for a Sun (Schröder and Smith 2008) and falls with the mass to the power 2.5 (unchecked), with three million years added that the heaviest stars do not go below: 8.6 million years at 20 Suns, as in the fit of Hurley and others (2000).
- A star is its mass at birth and its age, and one function of the core gives what it is now: its mass, radius and light, the widest and the brightest it has been, and the time since it ended. On the main sequence it keeps the radius and the light of its mass, and the light grows with the mass to the power 3.5 up to 20 Suns, between the 4.0 and 2.7 that Eker and others (2015) measure from 2.4 to 32 Suns, and 2.3 above (unchecked). Four stages follow, each counted against the main sequence: a turn toward the giant branch, 8 percent of it for a Sun and half a percent above six Suns (unchecked); a first climb, 6 percent for a Sun and less by the square of the mass (Hurley and others 2000); helium burning, 130 million years or 15 percent where that is shorter (Hurley and others 2000); and a last climb of 15 percent of that (unchecked). A star lives between 1.11 and 1.19 of its main sequence.
- Through those stages the light goes from twice that of the main sequence (unchecked) to 2500 Suns at the tip of the first climb for a star under 1.8 Suns, below the 2.0 up to which helium flashes in Hurley and others (2000), and that falls back to 50 when helium lights, near the 2730 and 54 that Schröder and Smith (2008) find for the Sun. From 2.4 Suns, above that 2.0, helium lights quietly, at 250 Suns of light times the mass over 2.5 to the power 2.5 and no less than 2.4 times the main sequence, and burns a fifth below that (unchecked). The last climb ends at 4000 Suns times the mass to the power 1.2, or at three times the main sequence where that is more (unchecked).
- The radius of a red star follows from its light and mass by the fit of the giant branch of Hurley and others (2000), 170 radii of the Sun at the first tip of a Sun and 240 at its last, and it is no cooler than 2800 K times the mass to the power 0.08 (unchecked), which keeps red supergiants near 3300 to 3600 K and under 1300 radii, where Levesque and others (2005) measure 3450 to 4100 K and up to about 1500 radii. While helium burns a star under 1.8 Suns stands a tenth warmer than that branch (unchecked). From four Suns it loops to one and a half times warmer and back, and from twelve Suns to three times, so one star is a red and then a yellow or white supergiant (unchecked). Above 25 to 45 Suns a star no longer turns red, between the new and the old limit of red supergiants (Davies and others 2018), and above 25 to 35 it ends bared to a core of two to four radii with 45 percent of its mass, a Wolf-Rayet star (Crowther 2007).
- What is left follows from the mass at birth. Under eight Suns (Smartt 2009) it is a white dwarf of 0.394 Suns and 0.109 of that mass (Kalirai and others 2008), with the radius of a cold degenerate body by the fit of Nauenberg (1972), 8700 km at 0.6 Suns and none at 1.44, the limit of Chandrasekhar (Bonolis 2017), and a light that falls with the time since by the power 1.4, as Mestel (1952) found: 23 000 K after fifty million years, 8000 after a billion and about 4000 after ten, near the 20 700, 8400 and 3950 K of the cooling models of Bédard and others (2020). From eight to twenty Suns, a bound between the 18 of Smartt (2015) and the 25 of Heger and others (2003), it is a neutron star of 1.2 to 1.8 Suns, within the 1.17 to 2.0 measured (Özel and Freire 2016), and 11.5 km (Abbott and others 2018) at two million kelvin, less by the root of its age over thirty thousand years, which puts Cas A and Geminga near what Potekhin and others (2020) list, with a day of 0.01 to 0.1 s that grows by the root of its age over ten thousand years (unchecked). Above twenty it is a black hole of three tenths of the mass at birth (chosen for play), which gives 6 to 18 Suns, about the 6 to 16 measured (McClintock and others 2014), as wide as its horizon, with the temperature and the light of Hawking, which are next to nothing, and a day of milliseconds from a spin within the range they measure.
- A giant turns as slowly as it has swollen, its day growing with its radius (chosen for play), and a cool one has braked as a Sun does: its day is no shorter than 2.2 times the time its convection takes to turn over (chosen for play). A white dwarf turns in a quarter of a day to two and a quarter (Hermes and others 2017).
- A star takes the turning its planets lose to its tide, laid in four parts at the times a quarter, half, three quarters and all of it had passed (chosen for play). On the main sequence a part spins up a body of 0.07 of its mass times the square of its radius, as the Sun is, and the star then brakes from that day as a younger star of that day would. Past the main sequence the parts stay, in 0.15 of the mass times the square of the radius as it is now (unchecked), no faster than the turning at which a star holds together. A white dwarf keeps none of it. A Sun of 4.6 billion years that swallowed a Jupiter from 0.02 AU would turn in three and a half days instead of twenty-six, one that swallowed it at 0.3 billion years turns within a day of its own by four billion, and a giant that swallows one turns several times faster than it would, as some do (Carlberg and others 2011).
- The name of a star is read off its physics like any other. A star-born body is a black hole where it is no wider than one and a half horizons, a neutron star above 10^13 kg/m³ and a white dwarf above 10^7 (chosen for play). Otherwise its color follows from its temperature, blue-white from 11 000 K and blue from 30 000, near where Pecaut and Mamajek (2013) begin the B and O types, and its size from its radius over that of the main sequence at its mass: a star under 1.3 times, a subgiant under 3 and a giant beyond, a supergiant from eight Suns, and a Wolf-Rayet star under 0.7 (chosen for play).
- In a system with no home a draw from a sixth stream of random numbers, so that a system whose star is left in its prime stays what it was, settles when in its life the star is met. Four in a hundred have a heavy star of 2.5 to 60 Suns, mostly the lighter ones, met between a twentieth of its life and 1.6 of it (chosen for play): half still burn, one in ten is a giant or a supergiant and two in five are a fresh remnant. Three in a hundred have a star of 1 to 2.5 Suns met between its main sequence and its end, and seven a star of 1 to 8 Suns met after its end, up to twelve billion years (chosen for play). Three more are met young, between 300 thousand and 100 million years, evenly by the logarithm (chosen for play). The rest are in their prime as before. The generator page may give the age, with the mass or without.
- Those shares are chosen for play, not counted. Near the Sun about six stars in a hundred are white dwarfs and under one is a giant, which the generator keeps close to; within 25 parsecs Golovin and others (2023) count 5.0 and 0.4 in a hundred. A blue-white star is one in a thousand there and a blue one or a supergiant one in millions (LeDrew 2001; unchecked for supergiants); they are made about one in seventy, one in a hundred and fifty and one in a thousand, so that they are met at all (chosen for play). A star in a thousand there is younger than ten million years, as stars form at a nearly even rate over the 11 billion years of the disk (Binney and others 2000), and three in a hundred are made young so that disks are met (chosen for play). Neutron stars and black holes are about as rare as in the Galaxy, two in a thousand each (unchecked), but only young ones are made, within six tenths of the life of their star after its end (chosen for play), where most real ones are billions of years old, cold and dark.
- Before the main sequence a star is wider by the cube root of one plus a third of the time still to go over the time gone: 2.4 radii for a Sun at a million years, 1.26 at ten million and its own at forty million, when it settles, near the 2.4 and 1.2 radii and the settling by forty million years of the models of Baraffe and others (2015). That time falls with the mass to the power 2.5 above a Sun (unchecked) and grows with its inverse to the power 1.4 below, a billion years at a tenth of a Sun (Baraffe and others 2015). The temperature falls with the width to the power 1.175 and stops at 4300 K times the mass to the power 0.16, the track of Hayashi (1961), so a young Sun gives 1.8 of its light at a million years and half of it at ten million, and a red dwarf keeps its temperature and only shrinks, as in the models of Baraffe and others (2015). A star of three Suns or more is never dimmer than it will be (unchecked). An age under a hundred thousand years is read as that (chosen for play). A young star turns in the day of a fast star times its width, and is named a T Tauri star while it is 1.3 times its settled radius or more (chosen for play), a Herbig star from two Suns, within the 1.5 to 10 Suns of Brittain and others (2023). The widest radius and the brightest light of a star are counted from the main sequence on, so its youth swallows nothing and costs no body its air or its ice.
- A system is laid once, as it was born, and then read at an age. The seed lays it around its stars in their prime, at the age the system would have if nothing were said of its star: the stars and their orbits, the ladder, the frost line, what a body is made of and the gas a young star took from it, its moons, its ring, its name and every angle. None of that is drawn again for another age, so one seed is one system at any age and the generator page can follow it through the life of its star. A system with a home is always read at the age it was laid for.
- Reading at an age gives each star what it is by then and each body its place and its state. Orbits widen by the mass at birth over the mass now, as orbits do around a star that sheds mass slowly, and a moon stands where tides have brought it by then. Light, temperature and air are those of that day, with a gas kept only if the body held it at the hottest it has been, under its star at its brightest. A body of ice that was warmed above 200 K then and is too light to hold steam has lost its ice and is rock, as the moons of a giant planet are around a white dwarf; a heavier one keeps its water as steam and is named a steam world while it is above freezing (unchecked). A ring of ice is lost above 200 K and a ring of dust above 1200 K (unchecked). A planet near enough to be held to its star by that age turns with it, and its oval is a tenth of what it was (chosen for play).
- A planet around a single star is drawn in by the tide it raises on the star, read from the main sequence on by the sum of two parts. The dynamical tide has a quality factor of ten million, between what the hot Jupiters measured give (Collier Cameron and Jardine 2018; Hamer and Schlaufman 2019), and shrinks the orbit by 4.5 times its pace, the mass ratio and the fifth power of the radius of the star over the orbit, over that factor (Jackson and others 2008). The tide of a convective envelope follows Zahn (1977) as Villaver and Livio (2009) use it: the envelope holds seven tenths of the mass of a star once it is three times as wide as on the main sequence and none on it (unchecked), its gas turns over in the cube root of its mass times the square of the radius over three times the light, the strength is 4.5 times the square of the year over twice that time where the year is shorter (unchecked), and the orbit shrinks by the strength over that time, the share of the envelope, the mass ratio and the eighth power of the radius over the orbit. Both are written for a star that does not turn, with the factors of Hut (1981) and of Jackson and others (2008) for an oval, which rounds 4.5 times faster than it shrinks under the envelope and 2.4 times under the dynamical tide. The core walks the life of the star in steps where the radius changes by four hundredths and the orbit or the oval by a twentieth, as a table of the star built once and only past the main sequence when asked. A planet falls when its nearest point reaches the star or the distance at which the star tears it apart, 2.44 radii of the planet times the cube root of the masses for a giant planet, the limit of a fluid that does not compress (Rappaport and others 2013), and 1.44 for a solid one, that of a rigid ball that keeps one face to the star. Around a Sun a Jupiter on a circle survives the whole life of the star from 4.2 AU, 3.8 widest radii, five Jupiters from 5.2 and an Earth from 2.1, where calculations that let the star lose mass on its first climb give 3.0 and 3.7 AU (Villaver and Livio 2009): here the star keeps its envelope and its pull until it ends. On the main sequence a Jupiter falls within 0.3 billion years at 0.02 AU and within 3.4 at 0.03, and one on a three-day orbit falls only as the star leaves it, which is why hot Jupiters are rarer around subgiants (Schlaufman and Winn 2013).
- What a star takes is gone for good. A planet that fell is listed on its star with its mass and the time since. Its hold shrinks with its orbit, and a moon whose far point lies beyond 0.49 of it, the edge of stable moons Domingos and others (2006) found, is torn away, as moons of planets that move inward are (Namouni 2010): from the first point of the path of its planet where that holds, it circles the star on its own on the orbit its planet had there, in the same plane and with the same apse, as a moon that slips out past the points of Lagrange keeps within a few degrees of it (unchecked), drawn by the same tide, and falls when the star reaches it. A moon whose path around its planet passes into the star goes in then, and the moons still around a planet when it falls go in with it. The star lists each of them as a moon of its planet. A planet that does not stand alone around one star, or a body around a brown dwarf, keeps the old rule: it was swallowed if its nearest point lay within one and a half of the widest radius its star has had (chosen for play). A planet that the widening has carried past the hold of its star against a companion, or inside the distance a pair now keeps clear, is lost, and so is a star whose orbit no longer nests outside the one within it, or the outermost star of a group that has outgrown its room, each with all that circled it. Neighbors on the ladder were spaced around the star at birth, and a lighter star lets each of them reach farther while a drawn planet closes on the one inside it: where two no longer keep the six Hill radii the ladder asks, the lighter of them is lost. Those limits are read at the age asked for and just after the end of every star before it, when the masses change, so what one star lost does not return under the next.
- A body is laid whole and read as far as it has grown. A planet that holds a hundredth of gas or more (chosen for play) took it from the disk, so it is whole within the life of the gas, 2 to 6 million years around a Sun (Haisch and others 2001) and longer by the root of the mass around a lighter star (Kennedy and Kenyon 2009), at a time drawn in the second half of that life, and its mass grows with the cube of the time: a core first and the gas at the end, by the share a body of that mass holds (chosen for play). Any other body is whole after 30 million years times the root of its year in years, the 30 of the Earth (Kleine and others 2002), half to twice that by a draw and never more than a hundred million, and grows evenly (chosen for play). A body is listed from a twentieth of its mass, and not while it would pass within four radii of the young star (chosen for play). A moon and a ring follow their planet: they go from nothing to whole as it goes from six tenths of its mass to all of it (chosen for play). A body that shares the orbit of another is there only once the system is a hundred million years old (chosen for play). What falls on a growing body warms it by its own pull as light would, which leaves a core that gathers gas molten. These draws come from a seventh stream, so a system older than a hundred million years is what it was.
- A young star takes in gas while its disk reaches it, that is, while the nearest of its disks still has gas: a hundred-millionth of a Sun a year at a million years for a Sun, more by the square of the mass and less by the age to the power 1.5, held at its value at a hundred thousand years before that (chosen for play), and half a power of ten more or less from star to star, drawn from the seed of the star. These are the numbers measured for T Tauri stars: the rate, a fall at the low end of the powers 1.5 to 2.8 and a spread of up to a power of ten (Hartmann and others 1998), and the square of the mass (Alexander and Armitage 2006). The core gives the rate and keeps nothing of it.
- A star that takes in a hundred-millionth of a Sun a year or more has a jet, faint at that rate and full at ten times it (chosen for play). About a third of the young stars watched show one, more among those that take in most (Nisini and others 2018), and by this rule nearly all do at three hundred thousand years, half at a million and a fifth at two. It is a ribbon along each pole of the disk that turns to face the eye, three times as long as the disk is wide, as thick at its foot as a quarter of the star and wider by 0.008 of the height (chosen for play), the three degrees measured (Burrows and others 1996), red as hydrogen glows. Knots go out along it, five and eight to its length, and cross the disk in about twelve years of its inner edge (chosen for play), as knots of 200 km/s cross 60 AU (Burrows and others 1996). The dust of the disk hides it by the sum that hides a point, so the far jet goes behind the disk and the foot is lost in the dark lane. Two things are a courtesy and not the truth. The jet is as narrow as it looks beside a disk of 250 AU, where the disks here end at 60: at its true width, 15 to 20 AU across at 50 (Cabrit and others 2007), it would stand over the whole inner system. And it shows only from more than 1.2 times the width of the disk away, in full from 2.5, and not along its own axis, because from inside it would be a glow across the sky. The wide winds of such disks are left out: they are seen in the lines of molecules and in the infrared, not by eye.
- The disk of a young star is what its bodies have not gathered yet, kept as belts are: a disk has a mass of gas beside its mass of dust and rock. Solids lie as in the nebula of the Sun of Hayashi (1981), 70 kg/m² at 1 AU times the mass of the star, as disk masses grow with it (Andrews and others 2013), less with the distance to the power 1.5 and four times more beyond the frost line, from the inner edge of the zone, or from where the wall of dust, lit face on, is at 1500 K and turns to gas, 0.07 AU times the root of the light of the star in Suns (Dullemond and others 2001), to 1.5 times the farthest orbit a planet may have (chosen for play), or to the hold of a companion. The dust thins out toward the edge of the disk, the reach or the hold of a companion where that cuts it short: by the cube of the distance over half that edge in the exponent (chosen for play), so nineteen twentieths of the dust lie in the inner half, three parts in ten thousand are left at the edge and no disk ends while it is still opaque. Every planet and every belt to be has the part of the disk nearer to it than to its neighbors. Around a planet what is left falls with the square of what the planet still lacks (chosen for play), and the planet clears a lane four Hill radii to each side (unchecked). Around a belt it falls with the square of the time left to a hundred million years, down to the belt, as the edges close in on it (chosen for play). The gas is two hundredths of the mass of the star, lies evenly along the radius and thins toward three quarters of the reach, within the masses and shapes of measured disks (Andrews and others 2010), loses nine tenths over the first four fifths of its life, and in the last fifth leaves from the inside out through a growing hole (unchecked), where measured disks clear from the inside out in a tenth of their life (Alexander and others 2014). With the gas the solids count as grains of ten micrometers, the size that carries the area of dust ground between a tenth of a micrometer and a millimeter (chosen for play) by the slope of Dohnanyi (1969), and the disk is opaque over its inner half. Behind the hole and after the gas they are rubble of a meter (chosen for play) that collisions wear down: one over the depth of a disk without gas is one over what its mass gives as such rubble, plus its age over 200 of its years. The dust near the star so goes first, a belt thins as the inverse of its age whatever its mass (Wyatt and others 2007), and a mature system differs with age in that alone. The 200 is chosen between the 40 that gives the dust of our own asteroid belt today and the 600 of the brightest disks seen (unchecked). Parts that touch are one disk, and the depth written for a disk is its mean.
- A disk is as thick as its grains are fast, and its height is written as one deviation of the grains from the middle plane over the distance. Dust rides the gas, which stands as high as the speed of sound over the speed of the orbit: 0.033 at 1 AU of a Sun and more by the fourth root of the distance, for 170 K at the frost line falling with the root of the distance (Hayashi 1981). The bell of the dust is six tenths as high as that of the gas, whose far wings are too thin to hold a grain: the lit skin then stands at 0.08 of the distance at 1 AU, 0.12 at 10 and 0.13 at 30, where 0.12 is measured (Avenhaus and others 2018). Where the gas is thin the dust settles below it, as Youdin and Lithwick (2007) give it, by the root of one plus the Stokes number of a grain of a micrometer (chosen for play) over a turbulence of 0.0003 (Pinte and others 2016): the finest grains carry the lit skin and settle last, so the disk flares and flattens as the gas goes. Where an edge leaves a share of the dust the gas has thinned with it, and the dust stands lower by the root of one plus half of one over that share less one (chosen for play): a tenth of the dust stands 0.43 as high and a hundredth 0.14. Without gas a disk around a star is stirred by the heaviest planet of its zone to twice the Hill radius of that planet over its orbit, 0.14 beside a Jupiter, which is the 11° of the asteroids. It is never under 0.04, the least for fine dust under the light of a star (Thébault 2009), nor over 0.3, and the stirring takes the hundred million years the belts take (chosen for play). A disk that nothing stirs is a ring: its grains meet gently and it is ten of them deep (unchecked). A planet thins the gas along its orbit to one part in one plus 0.04 times the square of its mass over the star's, over the fifth power of the height of the gas and the turbulence (Kanagawa and others 2015), so a Jupiter leaves a few thousandths and a Neptune nine tenths. The list of disks keeps the lane empty, and the client fills it by that share. Dust heaps where the gas is densest again, ten Hill radii beyond such a planet, where Pinilla and others (2012) find 7 to 10, up to three times what lies around for a planet that empties its lane, a number chosen. A belt holds nothing where a body would circle four, three, five halves, seven thirds or two times for each turn of a planet outside it, the gaps Jupiter leaves among the asteroids, within a part in 70 of that distance for a planet of a thousandth of its star, by the root of the mass and not at all under a part in 300 (unchecked).
- Two stars that may come to touch are read together as a pair: a star and its first companion, where that companion is a single star or a brown dwarf. The room a star has is its Roche lobe by the fit of Eggleton (1983), a share of the nearest distance of the two that follows from their mass ratio, 0.38 between equals. The oval of the orbit rounds as the widest either star has been goes from 0.4 to 0.9 of its lobe (unchecked), keeping the angular momentum of the orbit. A star that still burns turns with the orbit when the year is under twenty days, between the 10 under which most Kepler pairs turn with their orbit and the 30 beyond which fewer do (Lurie and others 2017), or the nearest distance under twenty of its radii (unchecked), the heavier star as well as the lighter, so the giant of a close pair turns many times faster than a giant alone and is as active as the RS Canum Venaticorum stars.
- When a star past its youth fills its lobe, what follows is read from its state then. The gas passes steadily when the giver is no heavier beside the taker than a limit and its lobe never closes to under half of what it was. The limit is 4 (Temmink and others 2023) for a star hotter than 7000 K (unchecked). For one cooler than 5500 K (unchecked) it is 10 to the power of how far the star is, by the logarithm of its radius, from three radii of its main sequence (unchecked) to the widest it will ever be: 1 at the foot of the giant branch, 2 for a Sun eleven times its width and 10 at the end of the last climb. That is the shape Temmink and others (2023) found, and the second condition holds the ratio under 2.2 to 2.5, the top of the 1.5 to 2.2 that Pavlovskii and Ivanova (2015) find when no gas is lost, raised by the 0.3 that losing gas adds. The older limit of 0.6 to 0.8 from Hjellming and Webbink (1987), which population codes still use, would send most such pairs into a common envelope, and the double white dwarfs measured fit neither picture well (Nelemans and others 2025).
- While gas passes the giver is as wide as its lobe and has the light of its own life. A taker that still burns keeps half of what comes, a number between the bounds (chosen for play), and a white dwarf, a neutron star, a black hole or a brown dwarf keeps none (unchecked); the rest leaves with the angular momentum the taker has on its orbit. The orbit follows from that, and at each moment the giver has the mass at which its lobe is as wide as the star would be alone, falling no faster than its mass over its thermal time. The passing ends when the mass is down to the core: in Suns the sixth root of the brightest the star has been over 234 000 Suns, no less than 0.098 times the mass to the power 1.35, both fits of Hurley and others (2000), and no more than the dwarf the star would leave, and growing evenly to that dwarf over the last climb. The core is then a white dwarf, or for a star of eight Suns or more (Smartt 2009) a bare star of the Wolf-Rayet kind that ends when the star would have. A star that shrinks to half its lobe before that (chosen for play), as when helium lights, keeps what it has left and may fill the lobe again. The taker becomes the star of its new mass that has burnt the same share of its main sequence, less by the ratio of the masses to a power that goes from 0 at 1.1 Suns to 1 at 1.6 (chosen for play), around the 1.25 above which a core that mixes takes in fresh gas (Hurley and others 2002), and it takes the spin of a newborn star. For twins of 1.10 and 1.06 Suns on a 12.4-day orbit this gives 49 million years of passing, a helium dwarf of 0.32 Suns and a star of 1.45 on a 119-day orbit, where the fit of Tauris and Savonije (1999) asks 0.33 Suns for that period.
- Otherwise the companion goes into the envelope. The energy that holds the envelope, the mass of the star times that of the envelope over the radius and a factor, is paid from the orbit, and that factor times the share of the orbit's energy that goes into it runs from 0.25 at the foot of the giant branch, as Toonen and Nelemans (2013) need for the close pairs red giants leave, to 1 at the widest (unchecked), by the same measure as the limit. If the companion and the bared core both fit their lobes on the orbit that is left they stay there, hours or days apart, and the envelope is gone. If not the two merge: three hundredths of their mass is thrown off, as models of V1309 Scorpii give (Ivanova and others 2013), and the star goes on by the clock of the giant with the mass of both, takes the angular momentum of the orbit as it takes that of a planet, and lists the other star as swallowed. A black hole that merges takes the star.
- Two remnants draw together by gravitational waves, the fourth power of their distance falling evenly, and merge when one fills its lobe: two white dwarfs into one of their mass, hot again, or, from 1.38 Suns together, into a neutron star of 1.25 that is alone like any other (unchecked).
- A star that swells beside a pair, or as the outer star around one, is not followed with them. When it reaches the lobe it had at birth its envelope leaves the system and its core stays on its orbit, a choice made for want of calculations of three stars in one envelope. The same holds for the one star left of a merger beside a farther companion.
- What circles the stars answers to this by the masses alone, as it does to a star that sheds mass: orbits around a star that grew close in, and those around a pair that shed widen. The tide on the planets of a star in a pair is walked along the life that star had. A moon of a planet whose star has grown is lost when its far point passes 0.49 of the hold of its planet (Domingos and others 2006). The moments at which the limits between stars are read include the start and the end of each passing, each envelope thrown off and each merger. A star carries the share of its lobe it fills and a list of what it gave, took and threw off, with the age at which each began and ended, and the generator page shows them with six decimals of a billion years so that the moment can be asked for.
- Every star that ends as a white dwarf is listed as having thrown off its envelope, alone or with a companion inside it. For fifty thousand years (unchecked) after, it carries a shell that has moved out at 25 km/s, the pace of planetary nebulae by their spectra (Jacob and others 2013); a shell has no weight in the core and moves nothing. On the drawn scale its radius follows the rule of the orbits.
- On the drawn scale two stars stand as far apart as their widths ask, which hides how much of its lobe a star fills. - When every body is made the tree is put in order of mass, so that no star circles a lighter one. A star that has shed mass may be lighter than a star listed as circling it, and the two then change places: the heavier takes the seat and the orbit of the lighter, the lighter circles it on the same oval turned half round and keeps the planets and belts that lay inside that orbit, and whatever circled the two from farther out circles the heavier. This is done from the innermost groups outward until it changes nothing, and the bodies are numbered anew, each after what it circles and in the order of its orbit. Nothing moves by it: over 12 900 bodies the places at any time stayed the same within a hundred-thousandth, the rounding of one angle. A system where no star has shed mass is left as it was, a system with a home among them.
- The life of a star leaves out much, on purpose. A star does not brighten along its main sequence and keeps its whole mass until its last stage. Between two stars no wind is caught, the star that takes gas does not swell under it, and a bared core of a third of a Sun or more (Han and others 2002) is read as a white dwarf at once, where a real one burns helium first as a hot subdwarf for a hundred million years (Heber 2016). A white dwarf does not draw in a red dwarf left close to it, so there are no novae, and no white dwarf explodes. The tide on a planet is counted as if the star did not turn and with no tide in the planet, and a planet that falls leaves no brightening, no dust and no lithium in the star. The limits between stars are not followed through the shedding itself, only read after it. Old neutron stars and black holes, planets formed anew around a pulsar and the dust a white dwarf gathers are not made.
- A young system leaves out as much. Bodies grow where they will stay and do not migrate, and their orbits are as round or as oval as they will be. The first hundred thousand years, when the star is still inside its cloud (Evans and others 2009), are not made. The light of a young star is not counted against what its planets keep, though a red dwarf, brighter for hundreds of millions of years, would dry the worlds near it (Luger and Barnes 2015). In the core the disk shades nothing and pulls nothing, a star too bright for planets has none, and two young stars too wide to stand apart are shown as one until they have shrunk.

- The day of a star follows from its age and its temperature. - A system is a tree of bodies with one rule of motion. A body, with all that circles it, circles the middle of the mass of the body above it and of those of its children that are nearer, each with all that circles it, and it moves that middle by its share of the whole. No node stands for a middle, and the rule does not ask what a body is: two stars, a star and its planet, a planet and its moon and groups nested in groups move by it alike. The core works out the places of all bodies at a tick in one pass from the middle of the system inward, on the drawn scale or the real one, and the middle of all the mass stays where it is. The period of an orbit counts all the mass the body circles that middle with: the body above, its nearer children and the body itself, each with all that circles it. The pull of the other bodies on a body is then what its path asks for, within a ten-thousandth for most and a few hundredths at worst, where neighbors tug at each other.

A moon torn by tides adds a ring of its own mass, around a body with no ring drawn too, with edges drawn from that stream, and a moon that reaches its planet whole adds none. Matter of a ring is left by one over the root of (1 + w/s)·e^(2t/w) − w/s after a time t, where w is the time in which the ring wears away, drawn for the body between 100 million and a billion years, around the 292 million in which ring rain alone would drain the rings of Saturn (O'Donoghue and others 2019), and s is the time in which it spreads, 0.0425 turns of its outer edge over the square of its mass in masses of the body (Crida and Charnoz 2012). A heavy ring so falls with the root of the time to the same mass whatever it began with (Salmon and others 2010), 10^20 kg around a Saturn after a billion years by this rule, and a light one only wears away. A ring is listed down to a density of 0.002 (chosen for play).
- A ring that would spread sooner than it wears away gathers a moon of a fifth of its mass (Hesselbrock and Minton 2017) once it has spread, just outside its outer edge, which stands for the distance at which its matter can hold together (Crida and Charnoz 2012). Inside the orbit on which a moon circles as the planet turns, the ring pushes the moon out to 1.59 of that distance, where it circles once while the edge circles twice (Crida and Charnoz 2012), and no farther than nine tenths of that orbit (chosen for play). Tides then bring it down, it is torn, and its matter is the next ring, for at most sixteen turns (chosen for play). Outside that orbit the moon stays and tides push it outward. A planet has one such moon at a time, the one of its heaviest ring, made from a stream of its own so that no other body changes, and has none where another moon stands within five Hill radii or where the moon would stand past three tenths of the hold of the planet (chosen for play). Around Mars a ring of a hundred-thousandth of the planet is followed by lighter ones after 16, 106 and 770 million years, and the last gathers no moon. In 600 made systems about 470 planets have such a moon in their prime, nearly all of them bodies in gas with a moon gathered from the ring they were left when young: around a heavy solid planet a moon comes down within a million years, and the turns are over long before its prime.
- A planet that holds a hundredth of gas or more (chosen for play) has a disk of its own until it is whole, made by the rule that makes the disk of a star, with the planet at its centre: from two radii of the planet (unchecked) to four tenths of its hold (Martin and Lubow 2011), cold enough for ice where the heat of the planet falls to 170 K (Hayashi 1981), its moons as the bodies that clear lanes in it, and the growth of the planet in place of the hundred million years in which a star's disk thins (chosen for play). Only the pieces that hold gas are kept, each stands a tenth of its width high at most and no higher farther out (unchecked), and there is no ice in it inside the frost line of the star. On the conventional scale it is laid among the moons as a belt is among planets. A body lists its ring first, and a ring is told from such a piece by the time in which it wears away, which only a ring has. While the gas of its star lasts a planet that holds gas stands upright on its orbit, so its disk and its moons lie in the plane of the disk it grows in, and every orbit around the star lies in the plane of the system. Poles turn to their drawn tilt, the moons with them, and orbits to their drawn planes between the end of the gas and a hundred million years (chosen for play). On the conventional scale the disk of a planet is drawn about ten times too wide against its orbit, as the orbits of moons are.
- A planet in gas with a moon born beyond three of its radii (chosen for play) had larger moons before, which its disk dragged down (Canup and Ward 2006). For half of them, by a draw from the stream of the planet, the last one came apart as the gas went, as Canup (2010) proposes for Saturn, and the planet has a ring of a millionth to a hundred-thousandth of its mass from the day it is whole (chosen for play). It spreads, gathers a moon and wears away as any ring does.
- Over 1200 made systems 46 bodies in gas in a hundred have a ring in their prime, 23 a dense one of 0.2 or more, 15 a thin one and 8 one under 0.02, where 45 had a dense one by the earlier draw. Of solid planets 9 in a hundred have a ring, and of moons 2. Laying a system takes 3.4 ms with a home and 4.6 ms without. The two moons at the rim of a ring are there from the first break of their planet on, whether or not its ring has lasted.
- The stretch of an orbit around the star is mostly under 0.05, as in systems of several planets, whose mean is 0.04 (Xie and others 2016), and at most 0.15 (chosen for play). Six orbits in a hundred are stirred, up to 0.6, but an orbit never takes more than eight tenths of the room to the next rung of the ladder, so neighbors cannot cross (chosen for play). Twenty systems in a hundred with no home are scattered (chosen for play), as most systems with giants once were (Raymond and others 2010; Veras and Raymond 2012): four rungs in ten keep a planet, no belt is left, and six planets in ten are stirred (chosen for play). A body is given the light of its mean distance, and holds moons and a ring by its pull at the nearest point. The home is stretched by no more than 0.03 (chosen for play).
- Neighbors on the ladder keep a gap between the farthest point of the inner one and the nearest point of the outer one of no less than six Hill radii of the two together, taken at the mean of their orbits (chosen for play). Jupiter and Saturn keep six and a half. Where the orbits as drawn leave less, the outer one is made rounder, and where a round orbit still falls short it is moved outward, and every rung and belt beyond it by the same share, so the ladder widens where heavy bodies need room. A pair set right this way is given eight to ten radii and not six, so no crowd stands at the limit itself, where a chain of planets would not last (chosen for play). The ladder widens to three times at most (chosen for play). A planet that may not move, inside the home, inside the room a companion star leaves or past that widening, is made lighter until it fits, and is not made where nothing would fit, as beside a giant. Of a hundred planets five or six are moved, by 1.6 times on average, five are made rounder, three or four lighter and two or three are not made, and the outermost planet lies beyond the old edge of the ladder in one system in thirteen with a home and two in seven without.
- The plane of an orbit around the star is mostly within 5 degrees of the plane of the system, as in systems of several planets, whose mean is 1.4 degrees (Xie and others 2016), and five in a hundred lie between 9 and 46 degrees (chosen for play). A moon has a stretch under 0.02 (chosen for play) and circles in the plane of the equator of its planet while it is inside the Laplace radius of the planet, where the bulge of the planet outweighs the star, and in the plane of the orbit of the planet beyond it. That radius is the fifth root of the bulge, with the moons nearer in counted as rings, times the cube of the orbit of the planet and its mass over that of the star (Tremaine and others 2009): 8 radii for an Earth and 27 for a Jupiter, where the measured bulges alone give 8.4 and 29. Four large moons in ten lie beyond it, the moon of the home among them, as the Moon does. A planet held to its star has next to no bulge, and its equator is the plane of its orbit anyway. One planet in twenty-two turns freely with its axis leaning more than 50 degrees (chosen for play), and has no moon beyond that radius, as Uranus has none: an orbit just outside it, pulled one way by the bulge and another by the star, does not last, as Tremaine and others (2009) find for a lean of 69 degrees or more.
- Three in ten of the small moons of a solid body were caught, with a stretch up to 0.2 (chosen for play). Inside the Laplace radius their plane is any, backward as often as not (chosen for play). Beyond it they lean no more than 39 degrees from the orbit of the planet, forward or backward, because the star stretches a steeper orbit until the moon falls, which is why no far moon of the giant planets circles between 55 and 130 degrees (Jewitt and Haghighipour 2007).
- A moon always turns one face to its planet. A planet is held the same way when it is nearer its star than half an AU (unchecked) times the cube root of the mass of the star in Suns and the sixth root of the age over ten billion years, and a body of ten Earths or more at three tenths of that (unchecked). A body so held has its day equal to its year, its axis upright on its orbit and a tenth of the stretch it would have had (chosen for play). About one planet in four is held, among them every world of a brown dwarf. The home is not, whatever its star, and its day is the one drawn for it.
- The temperature of a body is that of the mean light over its orbit, and with it are kept the temperatures at the nearest and the farthest point of the orbit around the star, which for a moon is the orbit of its planet. Only the light of that star changes along the way, and the body follows it as far as the heat it stores allows: by one over the root of one plus the square of 2π times its slack over the year, where the slack is the heat a square meter stores for a kelvin over the heat it sheds for a kelvin. A solid body stores heat in its air, in fifty meters of sea where it has one (Williams and Kasting 1997), and in the ground as deep as the wave of a year reaches, and a body in gas in its uppermost bar (unchecked). On an orbit stretched by 0.5, bare rock of 280 K runs from 222 to 379 K, a sea world of 288 K from 273 to 313 K, and a giant far from its star hardly moves. The core gives the temperature at a tick from these and the place on the orbit. Air and sea themselves are still worked out for the mean alone.
- What the temperature leaves out was measured over 3000 made systems, half of them with a home, with the same reckoning of stored heat. The light of the other star and of a glowing host enters the temperature as a mean and does not swing. With a far second star that loses at most 0.9 K, since the other star gives no body more than 6 percent of its light. In a close pair the two stars circle the point between them, and the light on the innermost planets, at four to six of their distances, beats by up to a fifth either way with their period, half a day to two months. A bare world there would swing by up to 32 K, 37 bodies in 1751 by 5 K or more, and a home by no more than 0.3 K. They are barren and desert worlds, none molten and none with a sea, so the numbers would change and the picture hardly. A moon on an oval orbit close to a glowing host would swing by up to 54 K, 22 moons by 5 K or more, but tides would have rounded such an orbit long ago, and it is the orbit that should change. An eclipse of one star by the other is not counted, and a body does not lag behind its light.
- The beat of a close pair can be added to the temperature at a tick without a change to what is stored with a body: the light of the two stars where they stand at that tick, against their light as from the point between them, followed as far as the heat the body stores over the period of the pair allows. It waits until a rule reads temperature.
- Physics is in SI units and is rounded to five significant digits when the system is made.
- The conventional scale is worked out once and stored with the body. A radius is the real one, relative to the home body, raised to the power 0.45 (chosen for play), so a star is about eight times and a giant about three times as wide as the home. An orbit around a star, the second one too, is the real one, relative to that of the home, raised to the power 0.37, and the orbit of a moon grows with the square root of the real one (chosen for play). An orbit is then moved outward as far as needed for its nearest point to clear the farthest point of the body inside it with its ring and moons, and the nearest one stands at least 1.7 radii of the star from its middle (chosen for play). Where a body is wider than the one it circles, as a star beside a heavier white dwarf or black hole or a giant planet of a white dwarf, that room is counted from the wider of the two, so the wider passes no nearer than 1.7 of its own radii, and the next orbit outward keeps as far from it. A period is the real one, relative to that of the home, raised to the power 0.6 (chosen for play). A system with no home is measured against an Earth standing where the light of its star is that of the Sun on the Earth. A belt takes its place among the orbits by the same rule, between its neighbors, and where its inner edge is moved outward the outer one moves with it by the same factor, so a disk drawn farther out is as wide for its distance as it was. A body carries the drawn edges and the period of each of its disks.
