# Nature

The model of nature behind the generator of star systems: what it makes, by which rules and numbers, and what it leaves out. Each number says where it comes from: a published work, cited by authors and year and listed in `sources.md`, a check against known bodies, "chosen for play", or "unchecked" where its origin is not yet traced. A mark at the end of a sentence covers the numbers of that sentence that have no mark of their own. Constants of nature and what follows exactly from a law need no mark.

- A system is made by the core from the seed of the world, in a stream of random numbers of its own, so a change of the generator leaves the cells and regions of a seed where they were. The seed of the home body is drawn first. Names, the seeds of the other bodies and their places on their orbits come from a second stream, so a change of the physics leaves the names of a system alone as long as it has the same bodies.
- The core also makes a system with no home, for the generator page and later for other stars. Its star is drawn between 0.08 and 2.5 solar masses unless one is given, and seven times in a hundred its centre is a brown dwarf of 0.013 to 0.074 instead (chosen for play), the least masses that burn deuterium and hydrogen being 0.013 and 0.07 to 0.075 (Chabrier and Baraffe 2000). Its orbits number up to twelve and nothing on it is alive (chosen for play).
- A system has an age: 2 to 8 billion years with a home and 0.1 to 10 without, and for a star in its prime never more than four fifths of the time it spends on the main sequence, the least age being at most half the greatest (chosen for play). That time follows the fit of Hurley and others (2000) for the share of metal the Sun was born with, 0.0142 (Asplund and others 2009): 10 billion years for a Sun, as Schröder and Smith (2008) find, 1.1 billion at two Suns, 100 million at five, 9 million at twenty and never under 2.9 million. A home around a star of more than 1.5 Suns is thus younger than 2 billion years.
- A star is its mass at birth and its age, and one function of the core gives what it is now: its mass, radius and light, the widest and the brightest it has been, and the time since it ended. On the main sequence it keeps the radius and the light of its mass. The light follows the six fits of Eker and others (2018) to stars measured from 0.18 to 31 Suns, which carry the four of Eker and others (2015) down to the lightest stars, taking the highest of them below a Sun and the lowest above so the light has no step, with the fit at the Sun drawn through its light, 1.6 percent above theirs and within its error: the light grows with the mass to the power 2.03 up to 0.45 Suns, 4.57 up to 0.82, 5.74 up to 1.02, 4.33 up to 1.7, 3.97 up to 8.3 and 2.87 up to 20, and to the power 2.3 above, within a quarter of the light Pecaut and Mamajek (2013) give the O stars. The radius follows their fit up to 1.5 Suns, made to give the Sun its own, and above it the radius that their light and their fit of the temperature from 1.5 to 31 Suns call for, whichever is smaller, so the two meet at 1.54 Suns. From 0.12 to 0.91 Suns, where their fit is a sixth to a fifth smaller, the radius follows instead the fit of Boyajian and others (2012) to eclipsing pairs of K and M dwarfs, which meets theirs at both ends: 0.32 radii of the Sun at 0.3 Suns and 0.61 at 0.6, where their fit gives 0.26 and 0.52, and 0.177 for Barnard's Star at 0.155 Suns, where Mann and others (2015) measure 0.186. The temperature follows from the light and the radius: 3140 K at 0.3 Suns, 3890 K at 0.6, 4750 K at 0.8, 6400 K at 1.3 and 16 200 K at 5, near the 3320, 3950, 4970, 6480 and 16 200 K that Pecaut and Mamajek (2013) give. From 0.27 to 0.5 Suns it stands 4 to 10 percent under theirs, as the light of Eker and others (2018) there is up to a third below theirs, and Barnard's Star is 3040 K where Mann and others (2015) give 3230. Four stages follow, each counted against the main sequence: a turn toward the giant branch, 8 percent of it for a Sun and half a percent above six Suns (unchecked); a first climb, 6 percent for a Sun and less by the square of the mass (Hurley and others 2000); helium burning, 130 million years or 15 percent where that is shorter (Hurley and others 2000); and a last climb of 15 percent of that (unchecked). A star lives between 1.13 and 1.21 of its main sequence.
- Through those stages the light follows the fits of Hurley and others (2000) to the mass, taken for the metals of the Sun and never below the main sequence. The turn ends at the foot of the giant branch, at 2.5 Suns for a Sun, and the first climb where helium lights, at 2750 Suns for a Sun, near the 2730 that Schröder and Smith (2008) find, 3130 at five Suns and 25 100 at ten. Under the 2.0 Suns up to which helium flashes the light then falls to that of a star burning helium around the core its light at the tip gives, 58 Suns for a Sun, near their 54, and above 2.0 to a share of the light at the tip that grows with the mass, 0.37 at five Suns. From 13 Suns helium lights before the giant branch, so the turn ends where it lights and the light does not fall, the change spread over a Sun (chosen for play). While helium burns the light grows to that at the start of the last climb, 163 Suns for a Sun and 2080 at five, as the square of the time in its logarithm (chosen for play, within the 0.4 to 2.5 of their fit). The last climb ends at 4000 Suns times the mass to the power 1.2, near the 4170 that Schröder and Smith (2008) find for the Sun and unchecked above, or where helium burning ended where that is more.
- The radius of a red star follows from its light and mass by the fit of the giant branch of Hurley and others (2000), 174 radii of the Sun at the first tip of a Sun and 227 at its last, and it is no cooler than 2800 K times the mass to the power 0.08 (chosen for play). With that floor the supergiants cooler than 4300 K, drawn as the generator draws heavy stars, stand at 3630 K on the median, a tenth under 3470 K and a tenth over 4120, and reach 1310 radii, where Levesque and others (2005) measure 3650, 3550 and 3900 K and up to about 1500 radii for the K and M supergiants of the Galaxy. While helium burns a star under 1.8 Suns stands 6 percent warmer than that branch, as Schröder and Smith (2008) find the Sun at 4667 K when helium lights. From four Suns it loops to one and a half times warmer and back, and from twelve Suns to three times, so one star is a red and then a yellow or white supergiant (unchecked). Above 25 to 45 Suns a star no longer turns red, between the new and the old limit of red supergiants (Davies and others 2018), and above 25 to 35 it ends bared to a core of two to four radii with 45 percent of its mass, a Wolf-Rayet star (Crowther 2007).
- What is left follows from the mass at birth. Under eight Suns (Smartt 2009) it is a white dwarf of 0.394 Suns and 0.109 of that mass (Kalirai and others 2008), with the radius of a cold degenerate body by the fit of Nauenberg (1972), 8700 km at 0.6 Suns and none at 1.44, the limit of Chandrasekhar (Bonolis 2017), and a light that falls with the time since by the power 1.4, as Mestel (1952) found: 23 000 K after fifty million years, 8000 after a billion and about 4000 after ten, near the 20 700, 8400 and 3950 K of the cooling models of Bédard and others (2020). From eight to twenty Suns, a bound between the 18 of Smartt (2015) and the 25 of Heger and others (2003), it is a neutron star of 11.5 km (Abbott and others 2018) and of 1.17 Suns at eight to 2.0 at twenty, the range measured (Özel and Freire 2016), evenly by the mass at birth (chosen for play). It is 1.6 million kelvin as seen from afar, less by the twelfth root of one and its age over a hundred years, as a star cooled by neutrinos falls (Page and others 2004), and by one and its age over 600 thousand years, as it comes to be cooled by its light, a fit to the 32 neutron stars of weak and ordinary fields with an age that Potekhin and others (2020) list, which it meets within a factor of 1.6 on the root mean square: 1.4 for Cas A where they list 1.4 to 2.1, 1.0 for Vela where they list 0.66, 0.75 for PSR B0656+14 where they list 0.74, and 0.52 for Geminga where they list 0.49. No one curve comes nearer Vela, as the stars within a factor of three of its age range from 0.44 to 2.0 million kelvin: Page and others (2009) explain the warmest by envelopes of light elements and the coldest, Vela among them, by heavy ones, which the model does not tell apart. It is born turning in 0.04 to 0.56 s, evenly, which has the mean of 0.3 s and the spread of 0.15 s that Faucher-Giguère and Kaspi (2006) find, and the mean field they find, 4.5 × 10^8 T, brakes it so that the square of its day grows by 1.2 × 10^-6 s² a year: a day of 0.3 s at birth is 1.1 s after a million years. Above twenty it is a black hole of three tenths of the mass at birth (chosen for play), which gives 6 to 18 Suns, about the 6 to 16 measured (McClintock and others 2014), as wide as its horizon, with the temperature and the light of Hawking, which are next to nothing, and a day of milliseconds from a spin drawn evenly from 0.1 to 0.98 (chosen for play), across the range from near 0 to above 0.95 that they measure.
- A giant turns as slowly as it has swollen, its day growing with its radius (chosen for play), and a cool one has braked as a Sun does: its day is no shorter than twice the time its convection takes to turn over, the Rossby number at which braking stops (van Saders and others 2016). A white dwarf turns in a quarter of a day to two and a quarter (Hermes and others 2017).
- The inside of a star is read from published tracks, not reckoned. They are those of MIST 2.5 for the metals of the Sun and stars that do not turn (Dotter 2016; Choi and others 2016; Dotter and others 2026): 30 of their masses from 0.1 to 60 Suns, each cut to some forty moments from its first contraction to the end of its last climb, and a star between two masses is a blend of the two by the logarithm of its mass. A star is placed on its track by what it is now, as its name is. Before it settles it is placed by its age over the time it takes to settle, and on the main sequence by its age over the time it spends there. After it, it is placed at the nearest point of the track in radius and light, each taken over those of the middle of the main sequence, light counting half as much as radius. Where two stages cross, the stage its age gives counts as nearer by 0.3 in the logarithm (chosen for play), and a star that burns helium is read for its centre by its age through that stage, as its radius and light hardly change then. A track gives the share of the mass in a stirred core, the share in the stirred shell that reaches the surface and the share of the radius at which that shell begins, the share of the mass in a spent core of helium, the temperature and density at the centre, the moment of inertia and the apsidal constant. Shares of mass are of the mass at birth, as a star here keeps its mass through its climbs, where those of the tracks shed some. The generator page shows them for a star.
- Twice the apsidal constant is the Love number of a fluid body (Ragozzine and Wolf 2009). A star whose turning is q, the square of its rate times the cube of its radius over G and its mass, is flattened by half of one plus that number times q and has a bulge, its J2, of a third of the number times q (Leconte and others 2011): by 0.50 to 0.66 of q on the main sequence, where it was 0.7 for every star. A white dwarf has the moment of inertia, the Love number and the density at its centre with which the tracks end at its mass, from 0.52 to 1.02 Suns and as at the nearer of the two beyond (Bauer and others 2026): 0.186 of its mass times its radius squared at 0.5 Suns and 0.164 at 1.05. A neutron star has 0.237 of its mass times its radius squared, times one plus 4.2 times its mass in Suns over its radius in km and 90 times the fourth power of that ratio (Lattimer and Schutz 2005), 0.35 at 1.3 Suns, and the apsidal constant 0.0763 found for one of 1.4 Suns with the equation of state SLy (Hinderer and others 2010). A black hole has no inside here.
- A Sun of 4.57 billion years has its stirred shell from 0.720 of its radius up, where 0.7133 ± 0.0005 is measured (Basu and Antia 2004), holding 0.021 of its mass, where the model of Bahcall and others (2001) has 0.024. It has 16.0 million K and 1.67·10^5 kg/m³ at its centre, where that model has 15.7 and 1.53·10^5, and a moment of inertia of 0.0707 of its mass times its radius squared with an apsidal constant of 0.0158, where the tracks of Claret (2019) have 0.0650 and 0.0134. Turning in 25.4 days it has a J2 of 2.19·10^-7, where 2.18 ± 0.06·10^-7 is measured (Pijpers 1998). A star is stirred all the way down up to 0.3 Suns, where Chabrier and Baraffe (1997) find 0.35, with a moment of inertia of 0.21, where a ball of gas stirred through has 0.205 (Lai and others 1993). It has a stirred core from 1.2 Suns, where Bressan and others (2012) find one from 1.1, and no stirred shell to speak of from 1.4. A Sun of a million years has a moment of inertia of 0.201 and an apsidal constant of 0.135, where the tracks of Claret (2023) have 0.200 and 0.137; as a giant of ten radii it has 0.114 and 0.046, where they have 0.113 and 0.047, and at the tip of its first climb 0.062 and 0.022, where they have 0.062 and 0.023. A white dwarf of 0.5 Suns has 0.186, where theirs has 0.175 once it has cooled to 6000 K. Read back at the moments and masses the table leaves out, it is within 0.03 of a share of mass or radius and 0.002 of the moment of inertia nine times in ten on the main sequence, within 0.04 and 0.008 before a star settles, 0.10 and 0.006 on the first climb, 0.02 and 0.035 on the last, and 0.19 and 0.025 while helium burns. Over 1200 made systems, half of them with a home, the stars have swallowed the same 83 planets as with the numbers this replaced, and are flattened by a quarter less on average.
- A star takes the turning its planets lose to its tide, laid in four parts at the times a quarter, half, three quarters and all of it had passed (chosen for play). On the main sequence a part spins up the star with the moment of inertia its inside has then, 0.07 of its mass times the square of its radius for a Sun, and the star then brakes from that day as a younger star of that day would. Past the main sequence the parts stay, in the moment of inertia of the star as it is now, 0.12 for a Sun of seven radii, no faster than the turning at which a star holds together. A white dwarf keeps none of it. A Sun of 4.6 billion years that swallowed a Jupiter from 0.02 AU would turn in three and a half days instead of twenty-six, one that swallowed it at 0.3 billion years turns within a day of its own by four billion, and a giant of seven radii that swallows one turns one and a half times faster than it would and a subgiant of two radii nearly three times, as some giants turn faster than their kind (Carlberg and others 2011).
- The name of a star is read off its physics like any other. A star-born body is a black hole where it is no wider than one and a half horizons, a neutron star above 10^13 kg/m³ and a white dwarf above 10^7 (chosen for play). Otherwise its color follows from its temperature, blue-white from 11 000 K and blue from 30 000, near where Pecaut and Mamajek (2013) begin the B and O types, and its size from its radius over that of the main sequence at its mass: a star under 1.3 times, a subgiant under 3 and a giant beyond, a supergiant from eight Suns, and under 0.7 a hot subdwarf below two Suns and a Wolf-Rayet star above (chosen for play).
- In a system with no home a draw from a sixth stream of random numbers, so that a system whose star is left in its prime stays what it was, settles when in its life the star is met. Four in a hundred have a heavy star of 2.5 to 60 Suns, mostly the lighter ones, met between a twentieth of its life and 1.6 of it (chosen for play): half still burn, one in ten is a giant or a supergiant and two in five are a fresh remnant. Three in a hundred have a star of 1 to 2.5 Suns met between its main sequence and its end, and seven a star of 1 to 8 Suns met after its end, up to twelve billion years (chosen for play). Three more are met young, between 300 thousand and 100 million years, evenly by the logarithm (chosen for play). The rest are in their prime as before. The generator page may give the age, with the mass or without.
- Those shares are chosen for play, not counted. Within 25 parsecs of the Sun Golovin and others (2023) count 5.0 white dwarfs and 0.4 giants in a hundred stars; over 20 000 systems with no home the generator makes 5.6 white dwarfs in a hundred, near that, and 1.9 giants and 0.7 subgiants, more than there, so that giants are met (chosen for play). A blue-white star is one in a thousand there and a blue one or a supergiant one in millions (LeDrew 2001; unchecked for supergiants); they are made about one in ninety, one in 190 and one in 700, so that they are met at all (chosen for play). A star in a thousand there is younger than ten million years, as stars form at a nearly even rate over the 11 billion years of the disk (Binney and others 2000), and three in a hundred are made young so that disks are met (chosen for play). Neutron stars and black holes are made three and one and a half times in a thousand, a share unchecked against the Galaxy, but only young ones, within six tenths of the life of their star after its end (chosen for play), where most real ones are billions of years old, cold and dark.
- Before the main sequence a star is wider by the cube root of one plus a third of the time still to go over the time gone: 2.4 radii for a Sun at a million years, 1.26 at ten million and its own at forty million, when it settles, near the 2.4 and 1.2 radii and the settling by forty million years of the models of Baraffe and others (2015). That time falls with the mass to the power 2.5 above a Sun, as the models of Baraffe and others (2015) settle up to 1.4 Suns, the heaviest they make, and grows with its inverse to the power 1.4 below, a billion years at a tenth of a Sun (Baraffe and others 2015). The temperature falls with the width to the power 1.175 and stops at 4300 K times the mass to the power 0.16, the track of Hayashi (1961), so a young Sun gives 1.8 of its light at a million years and half of it at ten million, and a red dwarf keeps its temperature and only shrinks, as in the models of Baraffe and others (2015). A star of three Suns or more is never dimmer than it will be (unchecked). An age under a hundred thousand years is read as that (chosen for play). A young star turns in the day of a fast star times its width, and is named a T Tauri star while it is 1.3 times its settled radius or more (chosen for play), a Herbig star from two Suns, within the 1.5 to 10 Suns of Brittain and others (2023). The widest radius and the brightest light of a star are counted from the main sequence on, so its youth swallows nothing and costs no body its air or its ice.
- A system is laid once, as it was born, and then read at an age. The seed lays it around its stars in their prime, at the age the system would have if nothing were said of its star: the stars and their orbits, the ladder, the frost line, what a body is made of and the gas a young star took from it, its moons, its ring, its name and every angle. None of that is drawn again for another age, so one seed is one system at any age and the generator page can follow it through the life of its star. A system with a home is always read at the age it was laid for.
- Reading at an age gives each star what it is by then and each body its place and its state. Orbits widen by the mass at birth over the mass now, as orbits do around a star that sheds mass slowly, and a moon stands where tides have brought it by then. Light, temperature and air are those of that day, with a gas kept only if the body held it at the hottest it has been, under its star at its brightest. A body of ice that was warmed above 200 K then and is too light to hold steam has lost its ice and is rock, as the moons of a giant planet are around a white dwarf; a heavier one keeps its water as steam and is named a steam world while it is above freezing (unchecked). A ring mostly of ice is lost once the body has been above 200 K and any other ring once it has been above 1200 K (unchecked). A planet near enough to be held to its star by that age turns with it, and its oval is a tenth of what it was (chosen for play).
- A planet around a single star is drawn in by the tide it raises on the star, read from the main sequence on by the sum of two parts. The dynamical tide has a quality factor of ten million, between what the hot Jupiters measured give (Collier Cameron and Jardine 2018; Hamer and Schlaufman 2019), and shrinks the orbit by 4.5 times its pace, the mass ratio and the fifth power of the radius of the star over the orbit, over that factor (Jackson and others 2008). The tide of a convective envelope follows Zahn (1977) as Villaver and Livio (2009) use it: the envelope is the stirred shell the inside of the star has, a fiftieth of the mass of a Sun on the main sequence, three tenths at twice that width and three quarters at five times, its gas turns over in the cube root of its mass times the square of its depth over three times the light, the strength is the square of the year over twice that time where the year is shorter and one where it is longer, and the orbit shrinks by the strength over that time, the share of the envelope, the mass ratio times one plus it and the eighth power of the radius over the orbit. Both are written for a star that does not turn, with the factors of Hut (1981) and of Jackson and others (2008) for an oval, which rounds 4.5 times faster than it shrinks under the envelope and 2.4 times under the dynamical tide. The core walks the life of the star in steps where the radius changes by four hundredths and the orbit or the oval by a twentieth, as a table of the star built once and only past the main sequence when asked, and finds a moment within a step, as when a moon is torn away, by halving it. A planet falls when its nearest point reaches the star or the distance at which the star tears it apart, 2.04 radii of the planet times the cube root of the masses for one wrapped in gas, which overflows its Roche lobe as a fluid whose mass lies near its middle, and 2.44 for a solid one, the limit of a fluid that does not compress, as rock has no strength to speak of at the size of a planet (Rappaport and others 2013), who take up to a tenth off that for an iron core. Around a Sun a Jupiter on a circle survives the whole life of the star from 3.4 AU, 3.2 widest radii, five Jupiters from 4.2 and an Earth from 1.7, where calculations that let the star lose mass on its first climb give 3.0 and 3.7 AU (Villaver and Livio 2009): here the star keeps its envelope and its pull until it ends. On the main sequence a Jupiter falls within 0.3 billion years at 0.02 AU and within 3.4 at 0.03, and one on a three-day orbit falls only as the star leaves it, which is why hot Jupiters are rarer around subgiants (Schlaufman and Winn 2013).
- What a star takes is gone for good. A planet that fell is listed on its star with its mass and the time since. Its hold shrinks with its orbit, and a moon whose far point lies beyond 0.40 of it, the edge Rosario-Franco and others (2020) find for moons circling forward over a hundred thousand turns of the planet, is torn away, as moons of planets that move inward are (Namouni 2010). Domingos and others (2006) put that edge at 0.49 from one start of each moon, and at 0.93 for moons circling backward, which here keep the edge of the others (chosen for play, one edge for all). From the first point of the path of its planet where that holds, it circles the star on its own on the orbit its planet had there, in the same plane and with the same apse (chosen for play), drawn by the same tide, and falls when the star reaches it. Namouni (2010) finds such moons flung by their planet onto ovals of 0.45 to 0.99 or out of the system, and here they would cross the orbits of their neighbors. A moon whose path around its planet passes into the star goes in at the first point where it does, a small moon at sixty degrees from another is shaken loose where its hold no longer keeps it, and the moons still around a planet when it falls go in with it. The star lists each of them as a moon of its planet, and only them: a moon that tides had drawn down, carried off or torn before, or that had left its planet, is not on the list. A planet that does not stand alone around one star, or a body around a brown dwarf, keeps the old rule: it was swallowed if its nearest point lay within one and a half of the widest radius its star has had (chosen for play). A planet that the widening has carried past the hold of its star against a companion, or inside the distance a pair now keeps clear, is lost, and so is a star whose orbit no longer nests outside the one within it, or the outermost star of a group that has outgrown its room, each with all that circled it. Neighbors on the ladder were spaced around the star at birth, and a lighter star lets each of them reach farther while a drawn planet closes on the one inside it: where two no longer keep the six Hill radii the ladder asks, the lighter of them is lost. Those limits are read at the age asked for and just after the end of every star before it, when the masses change, so what one star lost does not return under the next.
- A body is laid whole and read as far as it has grown. A planet that holds a hundredth of gas or more (chosen for play) took it from the disk, so it is whole within the life of the gas, 2 to 6 million years around a Sun (Haisch and others 2001) and longer by the root of the mass around a lighter star and shorter around a heavier one (Kennedy and Kenyon 2009), at a time drawn in the second half of that life, and its mass grows with the cube of the time: a core first and the gas at the end, by the share a body of that mass holds (chosen for play). Any other body is whole after 30 million years, the time of the Earth (Kleine and others 2002), times its mass in Earths and the root of its year in years, half to twice that by a draw and never more than a hundred million, and grows evenly (chosen for play): Mars is then whole in 4.4 million years and half grown in 2.2, where Dauphas and Pourmand (2011) find it at half its present size within about two. A body is listed from a twentieth of its mass, and not while it would pass within four radii of the young star (chosen for play). A moon and a ring follow their planet: they go from nothing to whole as it goes from six tenths of its mass to all of it (chosen for play). A body that shares the orbit of another is there only once the system is a hundred million years old (chosen for play). What falls on a growing body warms it by its own pull as light would, which leaves a core that gathers gas molten. These draws come from a seventh stream, so a system older than a hundred million years is what it was.
- A young star takes in gas while its disk reaches it, that is, while the nearest of its disks still has gas: a hundred-millionth of a Sun a year at a million years for a Sun, more by the square of the mass and less by the age to the power 1.5, held at its value at a hundred thousand years before that (chosen for play), and half a power of ten more or less from star to star, drawn from the seed of the star. These are the numbers measured for T Tauri stars: the rate, a fall at the low end of the powers 1.5 to 2.8 and a spread of up to a power of ten (Hartmann and others 1998), and the square of the mass (Alexander and Armitage 2006). The core gives the rate and keeps nothing of it.
- A star that takes in a hundred-millionth of a Sun a year or more has a jet, faint at that rate and full at ten times it (chosen for play). About a third of the young stars watched show one, more among those that take in most (Nisini and others 2018), and by this rule nearly all do at three hundred thousand years, half at a million and a fifth at two. It is a ribbon along each pole of the disk that turns to face the eye, three times as long as the radius of the disk and as thick at its foot as a quarter of the star (chosen for play), opening by three degrees at half its brightness, the cone that holds the jet of HH 30 (Burrows and others 1996), red as hydrogen glows. Knots go out along it, five and eight to its length, and cross the radius of the disk in twelve years of its inner edge (chosen for play), so they keep pace with the drawn orbits, where knots of 100 to 300 km/s (Burrows and others 1996) take one to three years to cross 60 AU. The dust of the disk hides it by the sum that hides a point, so the far jet goes behind the disk and the foot is lost in the dark lane. Two things are a courtesy and not the truth. The jet is as narrow as it looks beside a disk of 250 AU, where the disks here end at 60: at its true width, 15 to 20 AU across at 50 (Cabrit and others 2007), it would stand over the whole inner system. And it is drawn whole only from more than 2.5 radii of the disk away: from nearer than 1.2 only its foot shows, as tall as half the distance from the eye to the star, and between the two it grows to its whole length (chosen for play), because from inside the whole jet would be a glow across the sky. It does not show along its own axis. The wide winds of such disks are left out: they are seen in the lines of molecules and in the infrared, not by eye.
- The disk of a young star is what its bodies have not gathered yet, kept as belts are: a disk has a mass of gas beside its mass of dust and rock. Solids lie as in the nebula of the Sun of Hayashi (1981), 70 kg/m² at 1 AU times the mass of the star, as disk masses grow with it (Andrews and others 2013), less with the distance to the power 1.5 and four times more beyond the frost line, from the inner edge of the zone, or from where the wall of dust, lit face on, is at 1500 K and turns to gas, 0.07 AU times the root of the light of the star in Suns (Dullemond and others 2001), to 1.5 times the farthest orbit a planet may have (chosen for play), or to the hold of a companion. - A body born as a star is known by having no iron, rock or ice. What circles it is laid on the drawn scale as planets are, and its disks are belts.
- The core gives the same system as a survey: every body with its real radius and orbit in place of the drawn ones, in radii of the home or of the Earth where there is no home, its year and day in seconds, its rings and belts at their real edges and the sway of a body on a shared orbit as it was drawn, before the drawn scale parted the two. Every function of place and time then works on a survey as on a sketch, with seconds for ticks, and the scene draws either.
- The stars beyond the first are drawn from a fourth stream of random numbers, and the first companion from the first six numbers of the third, as the second star was before, so a system that stays single is what it was. Each pair followed together takes one more number of the fourth after all the stars are drawn, for what an explosion does to it.
- A body may share the orbit of another. It has the same parent, orbit and period, and stands ahead of the other by an angle that is a function of the tick: a middle, a sway either way and a period of its own. Sixty degrees ahead or behind with a small sway is a body at one of the two points where such a place lasts. Half a turn with a sway almost as wide is a pair that chase each other round one orbit. The one on the wider orbit falls behind, so the two orbits differ by a share that changes sign each time the angle turns back, each body moved against its mass, and the middle of the two keeps the mean orbit. Bodies on one orbit move the centre above them as one, and the scene draws one track for them.
- A place at sixty degrees lasts while the two bodies have less than a twenty-seventh of the mass they circle (Murray and Dermott 1999). Its swing takes one year over the root of 27/4 of that share, and the orbits differ by the sway times the root of three times the share (Murray and Dermott 1999). A chase lasts while the pair has less than 1/1200 of the mass it circles and keeps five and a half Hill radii of that mass apart (Ćuk and others 2012); a pair made here has at most two ten-thousandths (chosen for play). Its orbits differ by 1.6 to 4 (chosen for play) times the root of 8/3 of the share, or less where the two would come nearer than those five and a half Hill radii. The two then come no nearer than one over the square of that factor plus 2.5, in radians, and a round takes four thirds of a year over the difference, times one less that angle over π (unchecked), which gives about eight years for Janus and Epimetheus (Murray and Dermott 1999). A body on the orbit of a moon swings and chases by the year its moon has in the prime of the system.

A moon that tides carry past a twentieth of the hold loses its companions (chosen for play). About one system in 130 has a planet on the orbit of a planet of ten Earths or more, of a thousandth to a tenth of its mass and no less than a hundredth of an Earth, where a year there is longer than ten days (chosen for play). All are drawn from a fifth stream of random numbers, their names too, so the other bodies of a system stay what they were. - The lamps of a body are all the bodies of the system whose light an eye would see on it, down to a thousandth of the light of the sky (chosen for play): the glow of each by the square of its radius over the distance, which is the size of the orbit that parts the two in the tree. - A body wrapped in gas is laid the same way, with its gas above its ice, rock and iron and up to one bar. The gas is hydrogen with 0.275 of helium by mass, and its density follows the pressure along an adiabat, the path of gas that turns over, read from the table of Chabrier and Debras (2021): 25 adiabats that start at one bar from 40 to 10 000 K, each a tenth of a power of ten warmer than the last, at pressures a tenth of a power of ten apart up to 10^18 Pa. Below the 100 K at which the table begins an adiabat is carried up to one bar as a gas whose temperature grows with the pressure to the power 0.31, the power the table has there. From 140 GPa down the hydrogen is a metal: at that pressure fluid hydrogen was first seen to conduct as one (Weir and others 1996). Ice under gas is hot water. It follows an adiabat of its own from the temperature the gas has where the two meet, read the same way from the table of Haldemann and others (2020), 21 adiabats from 200 to 20 000 K at 1 GPa. Rock and iron stay as in a solid body.
- Which adiabat the gas follows is set by the heat of the body. Above the gas that turns over lies a layer that heat crosses as light, laid by the solution of Arras and Bildsten (2006) for an air whose opacity is a power of the pressure and of the temperature. Far up it has the temperature of the light of the star alone and of half the heat of the body, taken together as fourth powers, and the gas turns over from where the temperature would grow faster than the pressure to the power 2/7, as in a gas of molecules of two atoms. The opacity grows with the pressure and falls with the root of the temperature (chosen for play), and is 0.00298 m²/kg at one bar and 160 K, set so that Jupiter has 166 K at one bar, within the 165 ± 5 K measured (Lindal 1992). A layer of light deeper than 1 GPa is cut there, and gas that would stand higher than a sixth of the radius for each fall of its pressure by e is not held, so the body ends under it (chosen for play).

- A body has a magnetic field where something in it conducts and turns over. In a solid body that is its iron core, when it is stirred. The dipole moment is 4π times the cube of the radius of the core, 0.2, the root of the density of the iron over twice the constant of the vacuum, and the cube root of the stirring times the depth of the liquid shell (Driscoll and Barnes 2015, after Olson and Christensen 2006), and the field at the equator of the surface is that moment times the constant over 4π and the cube of the radius of the body. There is no field where the flow is too slow: its speed is 1.3 times the depth over the rate of turning to the power 1/5 and the stirring to the power 2/5, and that speed times the depth over 1.3 m²/s, the rate at which a field spreads in iron, has to reach 40 (Olson and Christensen 2006). A core that turns slowly for its stirring has a field of many poles with a dipole twenty times weaker: so it is where the local Rossby number, which grows with the root of the stirring and falls with the cube root of the depth and with the rate of turning to the power 7/6, is above 0.12 (Olson and Christensen 2006). They find 0.09 for the Earth with a fifth of the stirring its core has here, so the number is set to 0.09 for the Earth as it comes out here (chosen for play), and an Earth with a day of 1.3 days or more has many poles. A dipole is tilted to the axis by up to 12 degrees and one among many poles by 30 to 90 (chosen for play): the Earth's is tilted by 9.4 (Alken and others 2021), Jupiter's by 10.3 (Connerney and others 2022), that of Uranus by 59 and that of Neptune by 47 (Connerney and others 1987, 1991).

- The inside of a body leaves out much. A field is a dipole and its strength and no more. It does not turn over with a cycle, as that of the Sun does, lying within 45 degrees of the axis but for a year and a half in each turn (DeRosa and others 2012). The crust of a body keeps nothing of a field that is gone, a sea under ice does not answer the changing field of its planet, and the gas a field holds does not swell it. The dense slow winds of giants, the fast winds of hot stars and the wind of a neutron star are left out, so the wind of such a star is that of the law of the Sun or none, and a body in gas that turns slowly has the field of one that turns fast. - A solid body starts with a stock of eight gases: what its rock gave off, mostly carbon dioxide inside the frost line and nitrogen with some methane beyond it, its water, and what is left of the gas it formed in. - No more of a gas stays in the air than its vapor pressure allows, taken at the cold pole where the air is thin, so carbon dioxide frosts out on a cold desert and nitrogen on a far ice body. A moon has a stretch under 0.02 (chosen for play) and circles in the plane of the equator of its planet while it is inside the Laplace radius of the planet, where the bulge of the planet outweighs the star, and in the plane of the orbit of the planet beyond it. That radius is the fifth root of the bulge, with the moons nearer in counted as rings, times the cube of the orbit of the planet, (1 − e²)^(3/2) for the stretch e of that orbit and the mass of the planet over that of the star (Tremaine and others 2009): 8 radii for an Earth and 27 for a Jupiter, where the measured bulges alone give 8.4 and 29. Nearly six large moons in ten lie beyond it, the moon of the home among them, as the Moon does. A planet held to its star has next to no bulge, and its equator is the plane of its orbit anyway. One planet in thirty-six, by the draw of its axis (chosen for play), turns freely leaning more than 68.875 degrees, above which an orbit between 0.9 and 1.25 times that radius, pulled one way by the bulge and another by the star, does not last (Tremaine and others 2009), so such a planet has no moon there. Uranus has no regular moon there, and its caught moons circle far beyond.
- Three in ten of the small moons of a solid body were caught, with a stretch up to 0.2 (chosen for play). A body in gas is drawn up to three caught moons of 10^17 to 10^20 kg, one on average, beyond its other moons and its Laplace radius and inside the same 0.36 of its hold, with a stretch up to 0.2 (chosen for play), as the giants of the Sun have their irregular moons out to half their hold (Jewitt and Haghighipour 2007). Inside the Laplace radius the plane of a caught moon is any, backward as often as not (chosen for play). Beyond it a caught moon leans no more than 55 degrees from the orbit of the planet forward and 50 backward, because the star stretches a steeper orbit until the moon falls, which is why no far moon of the giant planets circles between 55 and 130 degrees (Jewitt and Haghighipour 2007). Above 39 degrees the star swings the stretch of such an orbit up to the root of (1 − 5 cos 2i)/6 at a lean i (Jewitt and Haghighipour 2007), and a caught moon is kept only where it stays inside the hold and five Hill radii from the moon inside it even then, which over 1200 made systems leaves about five caught moons for eight bodies in gas, at a seventh of the hold on average. A caught moon comes once its planet is whole (chosen for play).
- A moon turns one face to its planet once its tide can have slowed it by the age read: that takes its first spin times the sixth power of its distance and its moment of inertia, 0.4 of its mass times its radius squared for a mix and less for layers, over three times G, the square of the mass of the planet, the fifth power of its own radius and its yield (Gladman and others 1996). With a first spin of ten hours (unchecked), the Moon, Phobos, Callisto and Charon are held within fifty million years and Himalia, Phoebe, Sycorax and Nereid never, as seen, while Iapetus, which is held, would take 8.5 billion years and Hyperion, which tumbles, 3. Over 1200 made systems one moon in eight turns freely, five in six of them caught. A planet is held the same way when it is nearer its star than half an AU (unchecked) times the cube root of the mass of the star in Suns and the sixth root of the age over ten billion years, and a body of ten Earths or more at three tenths of that (unchecked). A body so held has its day equal to its year, its axis upright on its orbit and a tenth of the stretch it would have had (chosen for play). Nearly one planet in three is held, among them every world of a brown dwarf. The home is not, whatever its star, and its day is the one drawn for it.
- The tide a planet raises in a moon that keeps one face to it rounds the path of the moon: the stretch the moon was born with falls e-fold in the time that rounds the path of a planet, with the yield the moon has cold. Where the next moon out stands within 2 percent of the distance at which it circles half as fast (chosen for play), as the rule of order leaves many, the two are in step and the tide the inner one raises on the planet keeps its path oval. The heat that settles in the pair is n₀T₀ + n₁T₁ − (T₀ + T₁)(n₀L₀ + n₁L₁)/(L₀ + L₁) for the rates of circling n and the angular momenta L of the two and the torques T of the planet on them, each 3/2 of the yield of the planet times G, the square of the mass of the moon and the fifth power of the radius of the planet, over the sixth power of the distance (Meyer and Wisdom 2007). The inner moon has the stretch at which it sheds that heat with the yield of Io, at most the 0.02 a moon is born with. For Io and Europa that is 2.1·10^13 W and a stretch of 0.0020, where Io has 0.0041: a third moon in step, as Ganymede is, raises the stretch 1.64 times (Yoder and Peale 1981), which is left out. Over 1200 made systems a quarter of the moons that keep one face have a round path and one in eight is held oval by a neighbor.
- The temperature of a body is that of the mean light over its orbit, which it gets at a(1 − e²)^(1/4), a being the size of the orbit and e its stretch, and with it are kept the temperatures at the nearest and the farthest point of the orbit around the star, which for a moon is the orbit of its planet. Only the light of that star changes along the way, and the body follows it as far as the heat it stores allows: by one over the root of one plus the square of 2π times its slack over the year, where the slack is the heat a square meter stores for a kelvin over the heat it sheds for a kelvin. A solid body stores heat in its air, in fifty meters of sea where it has one (Williams and Kasting 1997), and in the ground as deep as the wave of a year reaches, and a body in gas in its uppermost bar (unchecked). On an orbit stretched by 0.5, bare rock of 280 K runs from 222 to 379 K, a sea world of 288 K from 273 to 313 K, and a giant far from its star hardly moves. The core gives the temperature at a tick from these and the place on the orbit. Air and sea themselves are still worked out for the mean alone.
- What the temperature leaves out was measured over 3000 made systems, half of them with a home, with the same reckoning of stored heat. The light of the other stars and of a glowing host enters the temperature as a mean and does not swing. A star outside the zone of a body gives one body in twenty more than 8 percent of its light, and a planet of a white dwarf with a bright companion up to nearly all of it, but as the body goes round its own star that light swings it by 5 K or more for only 14 bodies in 12 738, and by 24 K at most. Where planets circle a pair, the two stars circle the point between them, and the light on the innermost planets, mostly at 4 to 460 of their distances, beats by up to a quarter either way with their period, mostly three days to 760 years. A world there would swing by up to 16 K, 7 bodies in 1258 by 5 K or more, and a home by no more than 0.05 K. They are barren and desert worlds, none molten and none with a sea, so the numbers would change and the picture hardly. A moon on an oval orbit close to a glowing host would swing by up to 10 K, 2 moons in 197 by 5 K or more. An eclipse of one star by the other is not counted, and a body does not lag behind its light.
- The beat of a close pair can be added to the temperature at a tick without a change to what is stored with a body: the light of the two stars where they stand at that tick, against their light as from the point between them, followed as far as the heat the body stores over the period of the pair allows. It waits until a rule reads temperature.
- Physics is in SI units and is rounded to five significant digits when the system is made.
- The conventional scale is worked out once and stored with the body. A radius is the real one, relative to the home body, raised to the power 0.45 (chosen for play), so a star is about eight times and a giant about three times as wide as the home. An orbit around a star, the second one too, is the real one, relative to that of the home, raised to the power 0.37, and the orbit of a moon grows with the square root of the real one (chosen for play). An orbit is then moved outward as far as needed for its nearest point to clear the farthest point of the body inside it with its ring and moons, and the nearest one stands at least 1.7 radii of the star from its middle (chosen for play). Where a body is wider than the one it circles, as a star beside a heavier white dwarf or black hole or a giant planet of a white dwarf, that room is counted from the wider of the two, so the wider passes no nearer than 1.7 of its own radii, and the next orbit outward keeps as far from it. A period is the real one, relative to that of the home, raised to the power 0.6 (chosen for play). A system with no home is measured against an Earth standing where the light of its star is that of the Sun on the Earth. A belt takes its place among the orbits by the same rule, between its neighbors, and where its inner edge is moved outward the outer one moves with it by the same factor, so a disk drawn farther out is as wide for its distance as it was. A body carries the drawn edges and the period of each of its disks.
