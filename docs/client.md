# Client

How the browser client draws bodies, systems and the game, and how it is built and served. Decisions shared by all packages are in `architecture.md`.

- A body is drawn from its description alone. The client works out the relief of a body on the graphics card and keeps it as a cube map of heights and slopes: 512 texels a side for the home body, the star and the body in focus, 256 on a handheld, and 128 or 64 for another body. A pixel of a body then costs two reads of that map, and four on the home body from orbit, where clouds are added.
- A body has a map only while it is wider than 7 pixels on screen. The map is made when the body grows past 14 pixels, one body a frame, and dropped below 7. Until then the body is a speck: a point drawn as a small disc of the mean color of the body, lit by its stronger lamp and the sky, with its own glow added, never smaller than 3 pixels, so every body of a system shows from afar. A star as a speck has the mean color of its disk. The home body, the star and the body in focus keep their maps at any distance and are drawn as specks while narrower than 3 pixels. All specks are one draw call. Opening a system therefore costs the maps of the star and the home body however many bodies it has: in software 12 s for 12 bodies and 17 s for 17, where making every map at once took 16 s and 23 s.
- The relief exists twice, in the core in TypeScript and in the client in GLSL, built on an integer hash that both compute alike. Measured on every face of the cube for every body, the two agreed within 0.0003 of a height.
- The relief has a height in meters for one unit of its field: 92 000 over the pull of the body in meters per second squared, less on ice and down to 0.15 of that where the inside is still, and no more than a twentieth of the radius. The ground of a body in meters is that field, finer octaves of the same noise that fade out below the grain asked for, and its craters.
- Craters are layers of a lattice of cells in space, the pitch halving from one radius of the body. A cell whose point falls in a thin shell at the surface holds one crater there, between an eighth and a quarter of the pitch in radius, so a place is touched only by the 27 cells around it and a layer costs the same everywhere. A hash of the cell gives the place, the radius and how worn the crater is, and the share kept follows from the body: all on bare still rock, fewer under thin air, on ice and where the inside stirs, none under thick air, a sea, lava or life. Up to the bowl limit of the body, a diameter of 29 km over its pull and 4 km in ice, a crater is a bowl a sixth as deep as wide with a raised rim; a wider one is shallower with a flat floor. Craters narrower than about three grains fade out, so a coarse view leaves out only what it could not show.
- The map of a body carries craters in the same four numbers. Their slope is found exactly, not by a difference, and added to the slope of the relief, and their depth is stored along the radius, where a slope has no part, so the height of the map stays the relief without craters and what reads it elsewhere is unchanged. The shader of a solid body takes the depth out again for a body that has craters and colors by the height with it. On a float map the craters of the two sides agreed within 0.000004 of a height.
- The home body has two looks, and the distance of the camera blends one into the other. Near it the client shows a chart: colors by height and a hillshade lit from the north-west, with no night side. From farther away the star lights it, with day and night, clouds drifting over it and an atmosphere at its limb. Every other body has only the look from afar.
- The look of a body is a function of its physics, and the seed only settles details such as where a storm stands. There are three ways of drawing, and a body takes one by its mass and by whether it is wrapped in gas: a star, a body wrapped in gas, and a solid body. Inside each way the look is a set of numbers and colors that change smoothly with the physics, so bodies close in mass and warmth look alike and the cost of a pixel does not depend on where the numbers came from.
- A solid body has one shader for every skin: a ramp of colors by height, patches of a second color, cracks along steep slopes, frost from a latitude that the temperature sets, a sea where the description has one, clouds, and a deck of fog that hides the ground under thick air. The fog takes its color from the gases, orange from methane, pale yellow from carbon dioxide, white from steam and gray from hydrogen, and methane thickens it at a lower pressure. A bare rock, a rusty desert, a living world with seas and a shell of ice are sets of those numbers, and a body takes a blend of them by its air, its ice, its temperature and its life.
- A body turned fast enough is flattened at its poles by the share that follows from its spin, its size and its mass.
- A body that is not round is the same sphere pushed in and out in the vertex shader: stretched to three unequal axes and dented by three octaves of noise from its seed, by up to a fifth of its radius where it is wholly a lump. The shader finds the slope of the shape from two neighboring points, and a pixel costs what it cost before.
- A body is lit by up to two lamps: of the bodies it circles, up the chain to the centre, the two whose light an eye sees best where the body stands. A lamp has a direction and a strength, found every frame from where the two bodies stand, and a color fixed with the body, so the second lamp costs a pixel two dot products. The drawn strengths are worked out anew only when the light of a lamp has moved by a fiftieth or the temperature of the body by half a kelvin. The shadow of a ring on its body and of a body on its ring is cast by the stronger lamp alone. A third light has no direction: the stars of the sky, which light a body evenly and are all the light of a body with no lamp. The strength of each light is the conventional one of the core.
- A body that glows shows it. The glow has the color of a black body, taken a fifth cooler than the body and no hotter than 1400 K, so the hottest brown dwarf is no paler than the coolest star beside it, and its strength comes from the same scale as the lamps. A body wrapped in gas glows through the gaps in its clouds, more in its dark belts, the same on every side, and below 1600 K with the magenta that sodium and potassium leave of the light. Lava glows where it lies. A glowing body is a lamp to its moons, and it lights its own ring from the inside, less by the cube of the distance.
- Where rock melts on a solid body, the ground below a level is lava. The level follows the temperature of the place, which runs from that of the night side to that of the point under the star on a body with no air and is even under thick air, so an airless body has its lava on the day side and its lowlands melt first. Cool lava is dark crust with glowing cracks, drawn as lines of equal value of two readings of the map, and hot lava glows evenly. Fog over thick air hides it.
- Where the temperature of a body swings by more than half a percent over its year, the client asks the core for the temperature at the tick, and sets the level of the lava, the edge of the frost and the strength of the glow anew each time it has moved by half a kelvin.
- A body is approached from the side its stronger lamp lights, and where that would put the camera inside the star, as for a planet close to it, the camera stands as far aside as needed.
- The page `atlas.html` draws a sheet of bodies without a server: a row for each mass, a column for each distance from the star, a row of stars in their prime and a last row of stars earlier and later in life, each made by the generator of the core and drawn as in the game, with its class, mass, temperature and air written under it. The masses, the distances, the stars, the lives as pairs of a mass at birth and an age, `lives=1@11.4,15@0.0175`, the seed, the age of the system, the tick, the size of a picture, the distance of the eye in radii and the turn of the bodies are query parameters, and a star lighter than 0.075 of a Sun is a brown dwarf. It is the way to judge a change of the look or of the generator over the whole range at once. A sheet of 90 bodies with maps of 64 texels takes 16 s in software.
- The start page `index.html` is the generator. It makes a star system from a seed without a game and shows it in the scene of the game, which works with no home body. A system is made with a home, and is then the one a game with that seed gets, or without one, and the mass of its centre at birth may be given, from a brown dwarf to 60 Suns, and where there is no home its age, in a field or on a slider that runs by the logarithm from a hundred thousand to thirteen billion years, so one system can be followed from its disk to the end of its star. Seed, home, star and age are in the address, so a system can be reopened. A change makes the new system in the same page without loading it again: the old scene is let go with its graphics context, the address goes into the history so the back button returns to the system before, and presses that come before the new system is ready make only the last. A change of the age alone keeps the view: the body in focus, found by its name, the camera around it, the clock and the place in the panel. Where that body is gone with the years, the camera draws back to the nearest of its parents that is left. Time runs at a chosen speed or stands still. A counter in the top bar gives the frames of the scene drawn a second, timed over the frames that were drawn alone, and says the scene is at rest while nothing in it moves and it is not drawn again. Beside the scene is a list of the bodies, and for the chosen one every number of its description with what follows from it: density, gravity, escape speed, the temperature of a black body in its place, the reach inside which it holds moons and the one inside which it tears them apart, the lamps that light it with their light in lux and the strength each is drawn at, the looks its own look is blended from, and its conventional scale. The temperature of the moment and the lamps, each with its distance, follow the clock. A body that shares an orbit says with which, how far ahead or behind it keeps, and how often the two trade places. A button swaps the drawn scale for the real one: the same scene given a survey, with a clock in seconds that runs from real time up to a year a second. On a small screen the top bar keeps the next seed, the speeds, which scroll when they do not fit, and a menu with the rest, and on a narrow one the clock and the frame counter stand over the corner of the scene. On a narrow upright screen the panel is a sheet under the scene that folds down to the name of the chosen body. One button copies a link to what is drawn, to send to anyone: it adds to the address the body in focus by its name, the camera around it as last drawn, the clock and, on the real scale, the weather, every number in full, and opens paused on the same frame, since the first frames of a page take too long for running time to land where it was. Another copies the link with a line under it for a report: the speed of time, the sizes of the window and the scene, the pixel ratio, the kind of pointer, the frames a second, the build, the graphics card and the browser. The build is what `git describe` says when the client is built or served. Where the browser gives no clipboard, as over plain http on a phone, the text goes through a hidden field.
- On the real scale the scene keeps to the sky and does not turn with the body in focus, a body shows its lit look at any distance, the camera starts on the lit side of the home, and the nearest thing drawn may stand a ten-thousandth of a radius of the home from the eye. Clouds, bands and spots follow a clock of their own, a tick for every 72 seconds and at most 16 ticks a second, so they neither freeze at real time nor flicker when a day passes in a second.
- The state of cells lies over the chart as a veil: one mesh with a color and an opacity per cell. An owned cell takes the owner's color, brighter as it is built up and slightly transparent, with the hillshade kept, so the relief shows through. Unexplored land is veiled dark, and explored land out of sight is dimmed. The veil reads the cube map of the body, so it covers land only and ends at the coast. A cell whose flag is falling keeps its veil only around its middle, a smaller patch the lower the flag. A view carries the flag of every cell the seat can see.
- A cell on screen is the land nearest to its point, as far as a reach of about two cell widths. Cell shapes are the Voronoi cells of the land points, which the client builds from those points alone. A line between two cells is drawn only where it runs on dry ground. The client finds that with the relief function of the core, sampled along the side as map generation samples it, so a line stands exactly where the map has a land joint.
- Dark lines separate regions and bright lines separate holdings. A coast needs no line, so a selected region is also shown lighter. The line where a power presses is drawn in its color, dashed while the attack is only announced. White teeth along that line mark the contacts where a siege is under way. They point into the besieged cell and reach deeper as the siege nears its end. Where force presses a cell that holds, the line stays smooth and only the force on its label tells of it. Across a strait the line is an arc from the attacker's cell to the far shore, ending in a short bar on which the teeth stand.
- The scene is the whole star system: the star, the bodies on their orbits and a sky of stars. The camera always circles the origin and the world is moved around it. Near a body the frame is fixed to that body and turns with it. Farther out it stops turning, and farther still its middle moves from the body to the middle of the mass of the system, so one zoom leads from a cell to the whole system. The middle moves only where the body stays in view: while the camera draws back from 0.9 to 3.5 times the distance of the body from the middle of the system, so the zoom from a moon of a far star leads out through its planet and its star. A click on a body or on its name flies to it. The name of a body is hidden while the body stands within 70 pixels of the one it circles, and the name of a star only where it would cover the name of the star it circles. The body in focus always keeps its name. The wheel zooms over a name as it does over the scene. The veil, the lines and the labels of the chart fade as the camera leaves the home body. A frame is drawn only when something has changed, and the camera counts as moved only where it stands elsewhere than in the frame last drawn: at its nearest and its farthest the controls report a change every frame, by the rounding of the distance, while it stands still.
- The place and the spin of a body come from the tick through functions of the core. Between ticks the client moves them on by the time since the last view.
- Clouds and the bands of a gas giant move with the tick as well. Clouds are two layers read from the cube map of the body that slide along the latitudes in opposite directions at different speeds, so their shapes change as they pass each other. Nothing is stored or sent, any tick can be drawn at once, and a paused game stays still.
- A gas giant is drawn after photographs of the giant planets: straight bands along the latitudes, with curls and wisps kept to their edges. The bands are a table made once from the seed: for each of 1024 latitudes a color, a share of turbulence and the speed of the wind. Dark belts and bright zones take turns and narrow toward the poles, and the wind peaks at their edges.
- The cube map of a gas giant holds no relief. It holds what a flow of eddies, stretched along the latitudes, has done to the clouds: how far north or south each place was carried, and three cloud textures carried with it. The client follows that flow backward in eight passes, each one step that reads the pass before. One shader taking all the steps would be simpler, but a software renderer could not build it within 3 GB of memory. A pixel takes its color from the table at its latitude moved by the carried distance, so the edge of a band stays sharp at any zoom, waves gently where the air is calm and folds into hooks where it is not.
- The bands stream. A pixel reads the map at two places, turned about the axis by the wind of its latitude and half a cycle apart, and blends the two distances, not the two colors, so the pattern changes shape without ever showing double. Up to eight storms are worked out from the tick. A storm is an oval that drifts along its latitude. The bands bend around it as water bends around a stone, its inside is wound into a spiral that turns, and the largest leaves a wake of stronger turbulence. A pixel costs two reads of the map, two of the table and a pass over the storms the body has, two to five on most and eight on the heaviest. Where a storm stands and the two directions across it are worked out once a frame and not in every pixel, and the parts of the drawing of a solid body that a body wrapped in gas never shows, the chart, the glint of water and the fog, are left out of its shader. In software a frame close to such a body takes 65 to 85 ms where it took 86 to 100.
- The look of a body wrapped in gas is a blend of eight sets of numbers by its temperature and its mass. Cold and heavy, it has brown belts with a red spot, white ovals and blue-gray wisps along the equator. Cold and lighter, it is pale gold with soft bands, and light or very cold it is blue, deep with a dark spot where it is lively and pale cyan with hardly a feature where it is calm. Warmer, its clouds are white water, warmer still it is a clear azure, hotter it is nearly black, and above 1300 K it has patchy brown clouds of dust. A blend takes the storms of the nearer set and fades them toward the middle. A thin wrap of gas is drawn behind a veil: where gas is under a twelfth of the body its bands fade toward their mean and toward the gray of hydrogen fog, so the thinnest wrap looks like the fog over a solid body of nearly the same mass.
- A body wrapped in gas has no surface, and its edge is drawn as air that thins out, not as a ball inside a glow. The core gives the height over which its air falls by e, the temperature over the gravity and the weight of hydrogen with helium: 17 km on a Jupiter, a quarter of a thousandth of its radius, and over a hundredth on a hot light giant. On the disk a line of sight crosses more haze toward the limb, so the clouds fade into the color of the haze, lit as fog is, by one less the exponent of the haze of the body times the slant of the line less one, and are gone at the edge. Past the edge the haze goes on in a shell drawn from behind. A pixel of it takes the height at which its line passes the body, counted in those heights, and from it how much haze the line crosses: opaque where the line grazes the clouds and a hundredth six to nine heights up, where the shell ends. So a cold heavy giant has an edge as crisp as in a photograph, a hot light one a soft one, a tenth of its radius deep and more on the lightest, and the generator page gives the height of each. The shell is not drawn once the body fills the view. A solid body keeps its shell of air, four hundredths of its radius, over a ground that has an edge.
- The edge departs from a real one in three ways. The haze along a grazing line is at least five deep, so the top of the clouds never shows as an edge, where the air of a real light giant may be clearer. The clouds end at one radius and do not thin with height themselves. And haze scatters no more light forward than back, so a giant seen against its star has no bright arc.
- A body may carry a ring, and its description gives the inner and outer edge, the density and the share of ice. A ring is three to five bands, bright where it is ice and dark where it is dust. At a density of 0.5 or more the bands are broad with narrow gaps between them. Below that each band keeps its matter in a narrower strip, the root of the density over 0.5 of its width and never under 0.06 of it, so a ring breaks into ever finer and fainter bands as it thins. A ring is one flat mesh in the plane of the equator, inside the orbit of the nearest moon, and its density and color along the radius come from a table made from the description and the seed. It is lit by the sums the layer of a disk uses, taken once across the whole ring: light scattered once by a flat layer, by the height of the star over the ring and of the eye. Seen from the lit side it is bright where it is dense. Seen from the other side it shows the light that passes through, so its thin parts glow and its dense parts are dark. Its grains throw light back as a matte ball does, brightest with the star behind the eye, and a tenth of it forward as dust does. The body throws its shadow on the ring and the ring on the body, each found by following the ray toward the star, which costs a ringed body one more read per pixel. The camera stands farther from a ringed body, so the ring fits the view.
- A belt is a cloud of 300 to 1800 points by its mass, half as many on a handheld, in one draw call. Each point has a circle of its own, slightly tilted, and the shader moves it along by the tick, faster nearer the star, so a belt costs the processor nothing per frame. A real belt is far too thin to see, and the points only mark where it lies, as bright as the light of the star there is drawn. Where a belt is small on screen its points thin out, the smallest first, so that they cover no more than about an eighth of it and never hide what they circle. Any body born as a star may carry belts, and a belt circles the middle of the mass that lies inside it. The camera may draw back as far as 2.4 times the widest reach of the system, its belts and disks included, so a young disk ninety times wider than the orbit of its one planet still fits the view.
- The points of a belt lean by the height of its disk, and they fade in as its dust thins from a depth of a thousandth to a ten-thousandth, so a disk with gas has none. None is placed where the core says a belt is swept by a planet. Points are drawn after the dust and each is hidden by the dust between it and the eye, summed in the vertex shader in ten steps set closer near the point, from the table of the layer: a point behind an opaque disk is gone, and one in front of it is not, which drawing them under the layer could not tell apart.
- A planet in a lane between two disks with gas has a swarm, points like those of a belt that stand for the rubble it still gathers: 120 to 700 by the width of the lane, even in the logarithm of the distance and nearly flat. Each goes round at the rate of an orbit at its distance, counted from the planet, so those inside overtake it and those outside fall behind. A swarm shows only where the lane is open enough to see into, because the dust of the lane hides it as it hides a belt. A planet of three hundred-thousandths of its star or more also holds two camps of up to a hundred points each on its own orbit, sixty degrees ahead and behind, each point swaying about its place once in nine years of the planet: dust has been seen gathered there. Under three hundred-thousandths it holds none, and the number grows to the full hundred at three ten-thousandths.
- Dust dense enough to be seen, a depth of two hundred-thousandths or more anywhere, is drawn as a layer with a thickness, one draw call for all the disks of a star: a home system has none, and a wild one older than a hundred million years only around a heavy belt, for a few hundred million more. The layer is a table of 384 distances, even in the logarithm: depth, height, the light of the star as it is drawn there, ice and the glow of dust hotter than 650 K. The depth of a disk falls outward by the distance to the power 1.5. An edge is a bell 1.5 heights of the disk wide whose middle lies 2.5 of those widths inside the lane of its piece, and takes no more than four tenths of the lane, so the layers of two neighbors never run into each other. The outer edge is cut that soft only by the root of the share of dust the disk still has there: one that has thinned out by itself is not cut again. Where an outer edge, the thinning toward the rim or the lane of a planet leaves a share of the dust, the layer also stands lower, by the rule of the core for dust that settles and never under a twentieth, so a disk ends as a lens does and not as a wall. An inner edge keeps its height from the middle of its bell outward and is the wall the star lights. The lane of a planet between two disks with gas holds the share of dust the planet leaves, so only a heavy planet shows a gap, and the heap beyond it is a bell as wide as the disk is high there.
- A second table, 192 by 40, holds how much dust lies between the star and a place, by distance and by height in heights of the disk up to seven. The star shines from two points, four parts in 3π of its radius above and below the middle, which lights a flat disk as the limb of a star does. From this follow the bright wall at the inner edge and beyond a gap, the dark behind them, and a lit skin three to five heights up on a thick disk, higher farther out, that turns over where the dust thins: beyond that turn the disk lies in its own shadow, and from the side it is a dark lane between two bands of light. The table also gives each distance the height above which nothing is drawn, 4.5 heights or just over the lit skin, and around the highest of them an envelope that falls no faster with the distance than the steepest of them rises.
- The shader walks each ray through the layer in at most three stretches, split where the ray crosses the middle plane and where it rises out of the layer over the hole. A ray steeper than the envelope is walked only between the heights the envelope has where it leaves, found in three turns from above: the envelope cannot fall faster than such a ray climbs, so the bound closes in on the layer and never cuts into it. A stretch has 10 steps where the disk has a skin and 3 where it is thin throughout, 6 more for each time the distance from the star changes e-fold, and never over 28, set closer near the star. A step is not sampled but summed: dust lies across the layer as a bell curve, so the dust along a step and the light scattered out of it have exact sums for a flat layer, the classic ones for a ring seen from its lit and its unlit face. A layer a few meters deep and one a tenth of its width deep are therefore one case, and the walk ends when a hundredth of the light behind is left. Up to six bodies widest in view stop a ray as spheres. A ray that enters and ends inside the hole of the layer meets no dust and is dropped before the walk, as is every ray that ends on the star once the eye stands inside the hole: with a young Sun filling the view a frame in software then takes 80 ms, where it took 228. A body the eye stands within ten to thirty of its radii of keeps the dust six radii off, a courtesy to the eye and not a thing a planet does, so a planet deep in a disk can still be looked at. The layer is drawn after the glare of the star and before rings and tracks, so a disk seen from its edge hides its star. It is not drawn at the size of the page: all the layers of a system go into one buffer with a texel for every two pixels of the page each way, whatever the density of the screen, and that buffer is laid over the scene in one pass. Dust is smooth, so a quarter of the pixels or fewer shows the same disk, and the walk through the layer, by far the dearest thing in a frame, costs as much less. The buffer holds the root of the light in eight bits, which keeps the dark tones apart. A body would smear: its edge would be as coarse as the buffer. So a pixel within four texels of the edge of one of the six bodies takes only those of its four texels whose ray ends on the same body as its own, and every other pixel takes one read. In software a frame with a young disk filling the view fell from 256 ms to 139, and one with the whole disk in view from 280 to 110, where the scene without the layer takes about 80.
- Light is scattered once, forward by dust with an asymmetry of 0.5 and by rubble with 0.2, and shown by the light the dust gets, times the root of what is scattered, so that a haze a thousand times thinner than a surface is still seen. The light on dust is drawn as the light of the Sun on the Earth to the power 0.2 and at most three times that, where the light on a body is drawn to the power 0.1: a disk is then some ten times brighter at its inner edge than at 30 AU, where the real one is a hundred thousand times brighter, and the eye reads it as lit from its middle. Dust the star does not reach is drawn as dark as the sky and not darker. A body is lit through the disks of its lamps: the light of a star reaches it thinned by the dust in between, read from the same table, so a planet deep in a thick disk is dark and shows by its own heat. The lit dust carries streaks, a convention that shows which way and how fast a disk turns: nothing so fine has been seen in a real one. The light of a pixel is raised or lowered by up to three tenths by a pattern read at the place the light comes from, the mean along the ray weighted by the light. The pattern is two scales of noise in cells 4% of the distance deep and 9° or 16° long, and it is turned back by the angle the dust at that distance has gone since its last whole turn, so each distance carries it at its own rate, the rate of a planet there, and shear draws its patches into arcs. A pattern left to shear winds up without end, so there are two, half a turn apart, each new with every turn of its distance and faded to nothing at the moment it is renewed. The angles of the 384 distances are worked out in double precision each frame and passed as a table, because the product of a tick and a rate loses its fraction in the shader. The streaks fade where a cell would be under two or three texels of the buffer. A planet leaves two more marks on the lit dust, and both are a gain on the light of a pixel as the streaks are. The ring of dust heaped beyond the lane of a heavy planet is brighter along one side, a crescent about ninety degrees long and up to nine tenths brighter where the heap is at its fullest, that goes round at the rate of its own distance and not with the planet: such crescents have been seen, thirty and a hundred times denser than the rest of their ring. A planet of two thousandths of its star or more, twice a Jupiter beside a Sun, draws a spiral through its place, ahead of it inside and behind it outside, with a fainter arm opposite, that turns with the planet as one piece and fades within half an e-fold of distance: at six thousandths it is seven tenths brighter than the disk. Up to three crescents and two spirals are drawn for a star. The disk of a giant is thinned by the dust of the star between it and the eye, summed along that line once a frame, so one buried in the disk of its star does not show through it. Left out: light scattered more than once, which would brighten a thick disk by a fifth to twice; the shadow of a planet on the disk; warps, and the shadows that move across a disk. The glow of hot dust is drawn far brighter than the two parts in a hundred thousand of the star's face it really has.
- Measured where nothing but software draws, 1040 by 836 pixels with the disk across a third of them, a frame takes about 250 ms with an opaque disk and 320 ms with a thin one, against 90 ms with none and 80 ms with the flat sheet drawn before. A graphics card shades such pixels thousands of times faster, and by what clouds cost in games a disk across half of a 1080p view should take about half a millisecond on a middling card and a few on one built into a processor.
- The track of an orbit is the path of a body about the middle of the mass it circles together with itself. Where the body, with all that circles it, has three hundredths or more of that mass, what it circles has a track about the same point as well. A track fades as it shrinks on screen, between a radius of 12 pixels and one of 5, so the tracks of moons do not smear a far planet. The 256 points of a track are counted from where the body stands, crowded toward it by the cube of their number and stored as offsets from it, and worked out anew when the body has moved and the track is in view. The line then passes through the body however small the body is against its orbit, where points spread evenly and counted from the middle of the orbit would miss a planet at 30 AU by fifty of its radii and tremble by hundreds of kilometers. Specks are stored as offsets from the body in focus for the same reason.
- The name of a body behind the body in focus is hidden.
- The star is drawn hot and not lit, after photographs of the Sun: an even disk that darkens and reddens toward its limb, a fine grain, a few small dark spots and a soft glare. Its cube map holds no relief but a field of coarse cells, a coarse noise and a net of cells the size of supergranules, 30 thousand kilometers on a Sun, along which the brighter threads run.
- The grain is not in that map, because granules are far too small for it: about 1,300 kilometers on the Sun, four million of them. They are read from a three-dimensional texture of cells, bright inside and dark along their lanes, that repeats every sixteen cells. It is made once on the graphics card, 128 texels a side with its mipmaps, about 2.4 MB, and every star shares it. A star reads it at its own pitch: against the radius a granule is as wide as on the Sun and narrower on a cooler star, by the temperature to the power 1.3 over the gravity, a little over half as wide on a red dwarf. The sphere cuts through the cells, and the place read rises through the texture by a cell every eight ticks, so granules swell, split and fade where they are and nothing slides. Every 240 ticks the pattern dissolves over 48 ticks into a fresh one, read elsewhere in the texture, so the pitch never drifts by more than a twentieth. From afar the mipmaps average the grain away and the disk is even, as the Sun is in a photograph of its whole disk.
- Spots are not read from a map. A star has up to sixteen active regions, and the client works out from the tick where each stands and what it holds. A region is a group of up to six spots along an axis: a leading part, ahead as the star turns and nearer the equator by an angle that grows with the latitude and scatters widely, and a following part behind it. The leading part is one compact spot, sometimes with a companion, the following part is one to three spots, and a large group may have pores between the two. The larger part leads in seven groups of ten, and one group in twelve is a lone spot. A group emerges quickly and spreads to its length, its following spots fade first and break up as they go, and the leading spot outlasts them and grows rounder; the next group of the region rises elsewhere in the belts on either side of the equator.
- The sizes of the groups are those of the Sun. The size of a group is drawn from a log-normal law, so most are small and a few are large, and its length and its life grow with it. A yellow star at the height of a strong cycle carries about twelve groups at a time, six on the side in view, and its spots cover about a thousandth of a hemisphere, as on the Sun at an ordinary maximum; the many groups of the Sun too small to see are not drawn. An orange and a red star have slightly larger spots.
- A spot has a dark core with a ragged outline and, once it is large enough, a paler ring in which the grain is stretched into fine rays that creep outward. A number for each spot says how far its core is broken into a cluster of small ones, low for a leading spot and high for a following one. Brighter threads lie around a region, rise and fade with it and stand out toward the limb.
- A star has up to sixteen prominences, and they are not tied to its spots. Each is a curtain that stands on the surface along an arc: three thin sheets side by side that wave a little, so it has some depth from any side, and all the prominences of a star are one mesh. Its top is uneven and its body is threads, both read from the map of the star, and it may stand on legs with arches between them. More than half are quiet ones far from the spots, up to seventy-five degrees of latitude, lying mostly east to west, a sixth to six tenths of a radius long and 17 to 50 thousand kilometers high for a Sun; the rest lie in the belts of the spots, low short ones along any line and single loops. One in three ends by erupting, its middle rising a third of a radius as it fades. A prominence is drawn only where the line of sight misses the disk, so it shows at the limb, also from behind it, and not against the star. Against the disk of another star it leaves a faint dark red shadow, as a filament does through a hydrogen filter. At its densest it is as red as the limb, so it is always dimmer than the limb, and it is drawn after the glare of its star and covers it: three.js orders two transparent objects at one place by their creation, so the glare is made first and the mesh of the prominences has its bounding sphere at the center of the star. A star with no grain has none.
- In white light, as the disk is drawn, a prominence could not be seen at all. They are drawn as through a hydrogen filter, on purpose, and so is a thin red fringe of uneven height just above the limb.
- How spotted a star is follows from its Rossby number, its day over the time its convection takes to turn over. Its activity runs by the logarithm from 0 at a Rossby number of 3 to 1 at 0.13 and below, and is 0.13 for the Sun. Above the activity of the Sun the spots grow, up to three and a half times wider, and no core is wider than 0.07 of a radius.
- A star of low activity has a cycle of about 60,000 ticks, a quick rise over three tenths of it and a slow decline, and a draw makes the cycle of one star stronger than that of another. Whether a region rises is settled when its turn comes, by the level of the cycle then, so nothing appears or vanishes at once. Regions rise at 28 degrees of latitude when a cycle starts and at 8 when it ends, scattered by 6 degrees, on a yellow star, and twice as far out on a red one. Between an activity of 0.45 and 0.9 the cycle fades, and regions rise at any latitude, mostly high. Above 0.7 a star has a lasting cluster of large spots at one pole, and above 0.85 at both.
- A flare is a patch of threads far hotter than the surface, bright within two ticks and fading over eight. A star at full activity flares every few hundred ticks, the Sun a hundred times more rarely and far more weakly. Prominences are fewer at the low of a cycle and up to 1.8 times taller on the liveliest stars.
- The drawing departs from the Sun on purpose in four ways, all meant to be mild. At the low of its cycle a star keeps a quarter of its regions, about 300 millionths of a hemisphere under spots, where the Sun is nearly bare for months. Time is compressed: a region lives about 1400 ticks and a cycle 60,000, where a tick is 72 seconds on the real scale, against days to months and eleven years. And the liveliest red dwarfs carry spots on about 4 percent of the surface, less than the tenth to a half measured on such stars, because a star has sixteen regions at most. And a prominence is drawn several times brighter than a hydrogen filter shows it, where it has roughly a tenth of the brightness of the disk.
- Every shade of the star comes from one number, the temperature of a place relative to the surface: lower in the lanes between granules, in the ring and the core of a spot and toward the limb, higher in the bright threads. It is raised to a power for each color channel that follows from the temperature of the star, so a cooler place is both darker and redder, and a cool star has a darker limb than a hot one. The glare is worked out along the line of sight of each pixel. How far the line passes from the star gives its strength, so it follows the true outline of the disk wherever the star is on screen, and where the eye and the first body on the line stand along it gives the part of the glow that lies between the two. A body in front of the star hides the glare, a body behind it is veiled by it and a body beside it by half, with no edge across the body, and nothing jumps when the star leaves the view. Another star is the exception: its disk hides the glare wherever it stands, because a glow so much fainter than a disk could not be seen against one, and the dark limb of the nearer star then shows against the disk of the farther. Where the glares of two stars overlap their light adds up and levels off at the strength of a glare at the limb, so the corner between two disks is no brighter than the sky beside one. It is drawn as a square that faces the screen, or over the whole screen once the eye is within twice its reach, at a depth just behind the star, just before the body it veils or just behind another star on the line, so it never covers a disk. The six nearest of the bodies within that reach and of the other stars at any distance are given to it as balls, flattened as the bodies are. It takes its color from the limb and fades evenly all round. A pixel of the star costs two reads of its map, two of the grain and a pass over the regions that can reach the surface in view. Each frame the client leaves out the regions that are spent, those beyond the limb as the eye sees it and those that reach nothing inside the cone that holds the view, and hands the shader the rest packed together: about four of the sixteen on a Sun seen whole and one from close by. The spots of a region are looked at only inside the circle that holds them, so the cost does not grow with the number of spots, and a flare costs one more exponent while it is lit. A pixel of the glare costs no read, and one read within a twentieth of a radius of the limb, for the fringe. Where the disk of its own star hides the glare, from two pixels inside the edge inward, the pixel is dropped before the glare is worked out, unless a body stands before the disk there, and a pixel of a prominence is dropped where its line of sight meets the disk. Once the disk fills the view the prominences are not drawn at all, nor is the glare while no body stands between the eye and the middle of the star. The prominences of a star are one draw call of 14,000 triangles, and cost only the pixels they cover beside the disk. None of this changes a picture. Measured where nothing but software draws, in a view of 820 by 650 pixels, a frame with a Sun filling the view took 117 ms before these savings and takes 56 ms, and with the Sun seen whole 106 and 88 ms.
- The look of a star follows from its temperature, between eight sets of numbers from 2600 to 40 000 K, and its spots from how fast it turns. Above about 8000 K a star is white and then blue, has no grain and no spots, and is brighter at its poles while it turns fast, which a star swollen past three times the radius of its main sequence no longer does. The color of the star tints the light on every body.
- A swollen star needs no look of its own, because the width of a granule already follows from the temperature over the gravity: a red giant at the tip of its climb has seven cells to its radius where a Sun has five hundred. Cells that coarse are read softer, at a coarser level of the grain, pushed out of line by the noise of the star map, and overlaid with a finer layer of the same grain and with broad warm and cool patches, and their contrast grows by up to four times, as convection is more violent where gravity is weak. They change as slowly as they are wide, down to a tenth of the pace of the Sun. A cool giant turns slowly and is as quiet as an old Sun.
- A star denser than 10^7 kg/m³, a white dwarf or a neutron star, is an even disk of the color of its temperature with a dark limb and a glare, with no grain, no spots and no prominences. A cool white dwarf is therefore orange and not white.
- A black hole gives no light and is drawn by what it does to the light behind it: a black ball 2.6 horizons in radius, the shadow a hole casts, and a sky of stars bent around it. Each star of the sky is moved outward from the hole by the angle a point lens gives, with the Einstein angle of a hole seen from the distance of the eye, the root of two horizons over that distance, and made brighter as a lens does, up to three times. The same stars are drawn once more for each hole as their second image, mirrored inside that ring and dimmer. The sky is one cloud of points, so a hole costs one more draw of it and nothing per pixel. The bending is that of a weak field, wrong close to the shadow, and the bodies of the system are not bent, only the sky.
- A star that fills half of its lobe or more beside a companion is drawn in the shape it has: the surface on which gravity, the pull of the companion and the turning of the pair balance, found for each point of the ball, a drop with its tip toward the companion when the lobe is full. The frame of such a star is turned to the companion at every tick, so its spots keep their places as the pair turns. A full lobe is drawn as wide as the distance between the two makes it, and the glow starts at the narrowest width of the drop.
- Gas that passes between two stars is a ribbon that faces the eye, laid along the path a parcel takes from the tip of the giver under the pull of both stars in the turning frame, which swings it to one side of the taker. Around the taker lies a disk out to 0.45 of its lobe where that is 1.6 times wider than the star as drawn; the ribbon ends inside its rim, or on the star where there is no disk, as with a taker drawn large beside a close giver. The disk is a volume walked in sixteen steps along each ray, thicker toward its rim, with two arms that turn three times in a year of the pair, hidden behind either star by reckoning and not by the depth of the scene. Its color runs from the temperature at its inner edge to that at its rim, each the greater of what the passing gas releases and what the light of the taker gives, and gas under about 3000 K is drawn in the red of hydrogen.
- An envelope thrown off is a ball seen only by the light along each ray through its thin skin: nothing at its middle, bright at its rim, counted once from inside and twice from outside, green within and red at the rim while its star is hotter than 25 000 K, as the planetary nebulae are, and dim red otherwise. The camera may draw back far enough to see it whole.
- None of these three was checked on Direct3D, only on the software renderer.
- Flows are particles moving between the centers of neighboring cells. A particle stands for a fixed portion of cargo, so their number on a joint shows the volume. A strait is a dashed arc over the water.
- A garrison is a white square on its cell, and the top bar shows the force of the player against the room in all their garrisons.
- The panel holds the orders for the whole power, the army with its forward dial and the leaning, next to those for the selected region. A view carries, for each holding of the seat, the room the simulation is building toward, so the panel shows it whether the army placed it or the player fixed it. The garrison field of a region is empty while the army places its room.
- Roads are sand-colored lines between the centers of neighboring cells, wider with every grade. A view carries the grade of every joint the seat can see. A strait shows its road by the width of its arc.
- Labels and panels are plain HTML over the canvas. A panel is rebuilt only when its structure changes; between rebuilds only its numbers are updated.
- A region's label stands on the center cell of the region. Where the player holds cells in the region it stands on one of them instead: the cell nearest the middle of the largest connected group of the player's cells. A pressure label that would cover the label of its region is moved just above or below it.
- The force total in the top bar opens a report meant for debugging. One table lists the player's regions with the force resting in the garrison, standing at a front and on the move, the orders that ask for it and the region the moving force is heading to. The other lists, for every line of pressure the player takes part in, each pressed cell with its defense, the attacker's force facing it, the pressure on it and the most its contacts can carry, the progress of the siege and whether the cell can fall. The server works both out with the functions the tick uses, only for a seat that has the report open: the client asks when the dialog opens and tells the server to stop when it closes. An open report adds about 3 KB of JSON to each view of that seat.
- The matter total in the top bar opens the economy report, also meant for debugging. Three tables give one tick of the whole power: matter made and what it was built into, energy made, needed and met, force built and lost, each with what was lost on the way and with fallen flags. A table of branches shows the levels built, the share the cells would build now and what each branch makes. A table of regions shows for each the matter and energy made and used, what it builds, what building waits for matter, and what it sends to and gets from other regions. Picking a region lists its cells in the same way, with the progress of the next level. A region or a cell whose flag is falling says how many ticks are left, and the panel of a region says the same.
- The tick can keep a ledger: what every cell made, spent, needed and lost, and every hop of matter, energy and force. For the economy report the server runs the coming tick on a copy of the state with a ledger and sums it by cell, region and power, so the report shows the effect of an order at once, also while time is paused. A normal tick keeps no ledger. A report costs about as much as a tick. It adds 9 to 12 KB of JSON to a view, and about 0.9 KB more for every cell of the region picked.
- The `Snapshot` button in the top bar asks the server for a snapshot and shows the name it was given.
- The scene is drawn on demand: after a new view, a camera move or a resize, and continuously only while time runs and something on screen moves, flows on the chart or bodies seen from orbit. A paused game with a still camera draws nothing.
- On the chart a frame is about 32 draw calls and 65,000 triangles. The cube maps take about 17 MB for the home body, 8 MB for the star, which is kept at 8 bits a channel, with 2.4 MB once for the grain of every star, and 1 MB for every other body on a desktop, and 4, 2 and 0.3 MB on a handheld, and a visited body takes as much as the home body while it is in focus. Where the graphics card cannot draw into float textures the maps fall back to 8 bits a channel, and a gas giant keeps straight bands without eddies. The speed of the shaders on a real phone has not been measured: the development machine draws in software, where a frame takes seven times longer than before bodies had a look.
- One page serves desktop and handheld. On a narrow upright screen the panel becomes a sheet under the globe that opens on selecting a region, and on any small screen the top bar keeps time controls and totals and moves the rest into a menu. Targets grow where the pointer is coarse.
- The `Aim` button of an attack makes the next tap on the globe pick a cell instead of a region. The aim is marked with a cross in the attacker's color.
- Dragging turns the globe at the speed of the surface under the pointer at any zoom. A tap selects only when a single pointer went down and came up in place, so a pinch never changes the selection.
- On a handheld the client pauses the game when the page is hidden and caps the motion of flows at 30 frames a second. On returning after half a minute or more it opens a fresh connection before dropping the old one, because a suspended page can keep a dead socket that still looks open.
- The seat token is kept in the browser's local storage, so reloading the page returns to the same seat.
- In development the client runs on Vite at port 5183 and connects through its proxy to the game server on port 8787. Vite listens on 0.0.0.0 and proxies WebSocket connections on `/socket` to the game server, which stays on localhost.
- The generator, the flight and the atlas need no game server and are published on GitHub Pages at `https://hewabive.github.io/barycenter/`. A workflow builds them on every push to `main` with the Vite mode `pages`, which leaves the strategy page out, and deploys the result. The client is built with relative paths, so the same files work at the root of the game server and under the path of the repository.
